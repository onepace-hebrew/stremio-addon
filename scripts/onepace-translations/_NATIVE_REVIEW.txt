NATIVE REVIEW — required before `build` (the build refuses without it)

Why: the translator's own un-calque check runs with the English open, and the
anchored ear passes Hebrew that only makes sense if you remember the English.
A reader who never sees the English catches what the writer cannot.

1. node scripts/build-onepace-episode.js review-input <ID> > <scratch>/<ID>.he.txt
2. Spawn TWO fresh agents in parallel (not the translator, no English, no
   cues.json, no notes, no lint output) pointing at that file: reader A gets
   the prompt below, reader B the same prompt plus the REGISTER FOCUS
   paragraph. Take the union of their flags: one reader misses a lot (see
   calibration), and two identical readers miss the same lines.
   Calibration used general-purpose agents on Opus 5.5; recheck the numbers
   if the reviewer model changes.
3. With the English open, decide each flag: fix it in <ID>.he.json, or keep it
   with a reason (voice, house style, a reader false alarm). Fix by RE-VOICING
   the line in everyday words, never by swapping in one higher word: «אנשים
   שלי!» → «חבר'ה!», not «אנשיי!». Check every fix against the register
   question in the prompt (docs/translation-learnings.md §1.8).
4. Changed lines get ONE more blind read by a fresh agent (same prompt, only
   the changed lines plus 3 neighbours each side). Fix what it flags; that
   round is final, no third one.
5. Write <ID>.native-review.txt:
     line 1:  sha256 <shasum -a 256 of the FINAL <ID>.he.json>
     then:    one line per flag:  <i> | fixed|kept | <reason>
6. node scripts/build-onepace-episode.js build <ID>

Calibration (2026-10-18, IM_7, record in IM_7.native-review.txt):
- Round 1, one reader, full episode: 32 flags, 28 real.
- Round 2, fresh reader on 193 lines around the fixes: 14 flags, all real,
  12 of them on lines round 1 had passed. Hence two readers in step 2.
- Human gold (EL04), one reader: 26 of 614 flagged. Some of those are lines
  that learnings §1.5 calls good (בול פגיעה, בנאדם); the user hasn't ruled on
  that yet.
- Register gap (research 2026-11-03, reports/Natural Hebrew anime subtitles.md):
  the old prompt never asked about register and exempted "theatrical villain
  speech", so formal-but-grammatical lines passed. A fresh read of MA_1 after
  both rounds still found 9 lines, 7 of them bookish lines in formal mouths
  (Sengoku, narration), and the gate's own fix «אנשיי!» was one of them. Hence
  the register question, the dub test and reader B's focus paragraph.
A reader always finds something; that is expected. Don't loop until zero.
Human-translated episodes (folders with a " He.ass") are NEVER reviewed for
fixes or edited. Only read them, as calibration.

---- PROMPT (send verbatim, replace <FILE>) ----

You are a native Israeli Hebrew speaker watching an anime (One Piece) with Hebrew subtitles. Read the file <FILE>. Each line is `<number>\t<subtitle line>`, in episode order. Read all of it, in order, so you have the scene context.

Your job: list every line that a native Hebrew speaker would NOT say out loud in that situation. You're looking for lines that are grammatical and understandable but odd: unnatural phrasing, wrong collocation, a near-miss idiom (one word off the fixed expression), awkward word order, a wording that only makes sense if you imagine some other language behind it.

Also flag any line that sounds written, literary, biblical, legal or newsreader-like rather than spoken, even when it is grammatical and even when the speaker is formal, old, royal or a villain. Formal characters speak in complete sentences without slang; they do not speak book Hebrew. Typical signs: היכן, כיצד, מדוע, אינו/אינני, אכן, כה, כעת, אנא, ייתכן, חרפה, ציווה, אסיר תודה, כצפוי מ…, a yes/no question that opens with האם, suffixed possessives on everyday nouns (אנשיי, חבריו, אביך), a suffix plus של (הוצאתו להורג של…).

Test each line: read it aloud as that character. Would a Hebrew dub actor say it?

Do NOT flag:
- Spelling conventions (הכל, האלו, מידי, דיי, זו), these are house style.
- Character quirks: one character refers to himself as ווי instead of אני; that is intentional.
- Gender/number agreement, names, or punctuation, unless the phrasing itself is odd.
- Short shouted exclamations and attack calls (מה?!, לעזאזל!, אין מצב!, הו!).

Be strict but honest: flag only lines you are fairly confident a native speaker would find odd. Do not pad the list.

Do not read any other files. Do not look anything up. Do not edit anything.

Final answer: one line per flagged item, exactly `<number> | <the line> | <short reason in English>`, then a last line `TOTAL: <count>`.

---- REGISTER FOCUS (reader B only: append to the prompt above) ----

Read a second time with one question only: is this how people talk now, or how books, news and old translations write? Mark every line that a viewer would notice as "written": a formal word where an everyday one exists (למה/איך/איפה/עכשיו/בבקשה/אבל/תודה), an English or anime stock phrase rendered word for word ("As expected of…", "I see", "Well,", "Indeed", "Yes, sir"), or a long line built like an English sentence. Short, firm, polite lines are fine for formal characters; book words are not.
