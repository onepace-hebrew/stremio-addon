# Translation-studies and linguistics research on "translated Hebrew" (translationese) and register in Hebrew screen translation

Scope: what research says about (a) linguistic markers of elevated/literary or translated register in Hebrew dialogue, (b) the history of Israeli subtitling and dubbing norms and their shift toward colloquial Hebrew, (c) artifacts of relay/pivot translation through English, and (d) evidence-based rules for our translator.

Everything else is cited from abstracts, tables of contents or secondary summaries, and I say so where it applies. Paywalled sources I could not read are listed under Gaps. I also measured a few register markers in our own data. Those numbers are **my own measurement** and are labelled as such.

## Q1. What did Toury, Ben-Shahar, Ben-Ari, Weissbrod, Even-Zohar, Shavit, Sela-Sheffy and later scholars find about translated Hebrew dialogue being more literary or normative, and which concrete features did they document?

### Takeaway
For most of the 20th century, translated Hebrew dialogue was systematically more elevated than original Hebrew. Two forces drove this: a "norm of elevated language" (show off rich, old, written Hebrew) and interference from the source text, mainly calqued phatic elements such as vocatives, interjections and discourse connectors. The documented markers are mostly **lexical and phraseological**, not grammatical:
- rare or high words chosen over neutral ones
- stock written-language fixed expressions and Biblical allusions
- pairs of synonyms
- avoiding repetition by swapping in synonyms
- completing every elliptical fragment into a full sentence
- bound (synthetic) possessive and accusative pronouns
- calqued interjections and vocatives

The most relevant warning for an AI translator is Ben-Shahar's **"mixed artificial language"**: spoken-sounding words dropped into high-style grammar or into slightly bent fixed expressions. The result reads as neither written nor spoken Hebrew.

### Cited Findings

**Rina Ben-Shahar (Haifa)**
- **Corpus.** 180 plays translated into Hebrew between the late 1940s and the late 1970s: 125 from English and 55 from French. She compares them with original Hebrew plays. — [Ben-Shahar 1998, Meta 43(1):54–67](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar) ([full text PDF](https://www.erudit.org/en/revue/meta/1998/v43/n1/002059ar.pdf))
- **Three ways translators shaped dialogue** (same source):
  - (1) "standard and super-standard written Hebrew, ignoring the principles of spoken language";
  - (2) a "pseudo-spoken variety… calques of original text elements, or intra-Hebrew inventions… not based on authentic structures of spoken Hebrew";
  - (3) "authentic elements of the spoken variety arranged according to actual principles of spoken Hebrew", a mode that "has gained ground… especially since the seventies".
  - Modes 1 and 2 prevailed until the late 1960s.
- **Why.** "The Hebrew spoken in Israel was conceived of as incorrect language unworthy of use in literature and theatre and, for many, even unworthy of everyday use." Translated drama was "epigonic": it kept older norms after original Hebrew drama had moved closer to speech. Playwrights who also translated used "considerably different norms" in each role. — [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)
- **Loss of character voice.** "stylistic differentiation between different characters or situations, shaped in the source plays, were often erased in Hebrew translations, by means of elevating sub-standard and standard styles… The inevitable effect was a stylistic uniformity." — [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)
- **The "mixed artificial language"** (same source):
  - Spoken lexemes "were usually embedded in a high-style grammatical context. The result was a mixed artificial language which combined different stylistic levels… Such language did not conjure up an impression of authentic conversation even when the translator actually intended to convey such an impression."
  - Translators "impose on the lexicon the role of representing speech", with "minimal awareness… of other linguistic domains such as syntax, rhythm and intonation".
- **Calqued phatic elements are the clearest tell of translation.** Calques persisted "in the field of vocatives, expletives, exclamations and void pragmatic connectives, to the extent that their nature in translated plays is one of the major elements indicating that these plays are translated plays." She gives three causes:
  - (1) formal equivalence: translators render these elements "whenever they occur in a source text, even where their use in Hebrew is less frequent";
  - (2) treating language as purely referential, so expressive and phatic words get translated literally;
  - (3) gaps in the Hebrew of the time. — [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)
- **Calque examples** (same source):
  - "Hey, brother!" became «הלו, אחא!» ('hallo, brother'). אחא is an Aramaic-pattern high-style word "never used in everyday conversation", and «הלו» is used only on the phone.
  - "We still are." became «אנחנו עדיין.» ('we still.'), an artificial ellipsis copied from the English auxiliary verb.
  - "mon petit Maréchal" became «אלופי הפעוט» ('my tiny Brigadier'), even though פעוט is only ever said of small children.
- **The "rhetorical-stylistic model"** of the 1940s–50s, which partly survived into the 1960s–70s. The same model is documented by [Toury 1977](https://tau.ac.il/~tarbut/Toury/works/dts.html) for 1930–45 and by Weissbrod 1989 for 1958–80. Its principles, quoted from [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar):
  - (1) "Using grammatical and lexical elements possessing high stylistic value… the stylistic effect seems to be of greater importance for translators… than accuracy";
  - (2) "high-style fixed expressions" in place of "stylistically neutral free phrases";
  - (3) "redundant sequences of two fixed synonymous nouns, or verbs";
  - (4) "Avoiding verbal repetition… by using synonyms and even deletion";
  - (5) "complete, well-formed syntactic structures": completing ellipsis, "omitting void pragmatic connectives", replacing free word order with ordinary word order, asyndeton with syndeton, and parataxis with hypotaxis.
- **Examples of the model** (all from [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)):
  - Fixed expressions:
    - "with your brother" became «עם אחיך עצמך ובשרך» ('your brother, your own flesh and blood', with no counterpart in the source).
    - "Why you got to flit from one thing to another?" became «למה את פחז כמים?» ('unstable as water', from Genesis 49:4).
    - "Sounds rather like Daddy, don't you think?" became «הקול קולו של אבא, לא כן?» (Genesis 27:22).
  - Synonym pairs:
    - "Je veux bien" became «אני מוכן ומזומן» ('ready and prepared').
    - "She is a knockout" became «הבחורה הזאת — חזיז ורעם» ('flash and thunder').
    - "Ça n'a jamais existé" became «לא הייתה ולא נבראה» ('never was and never created').
  - Avoided repetition:
    - A repeated "moving" became four verbs, רוחש / זז / נע / זע (the last pair a bent form of the fixed expression נע ונד).
    - "Tell me… just tell me" became «הגד לי… רק תגיד לי», a written-register imperative and a spoken one used for variety.
  - Over-completion:
    - "Votre panier… vos provisions…" became «הסל שלך… המצרכים שנפוצו על הרצפה» ('the provisions which have been scattered on the floor').
- **Bent fixed expressions** (same source):
  - "Occupe-toi de tes affaires" became «דאג לעסקיך אתה ואל תתחוב את אפך לעסקיהם של אחרים». The vulgar spoken verb דחף was swapped for the high verb תחב, "an automatic stock replacement", and "the synthetic possessive morpheme (אפך) was preferred to the spoken language analytic possessive (האף שלך)". The result is "a mixed-style expression… [that] does not reflect authentic speech".
  - "Now, up your arse" became «את החוכמות שלך תחוב בתחת», with the same high verb inside a vulgar expression.
  - Deviating from a spoken fixed expression raises its style; deviating from a written one lowers it.
- **Conclusion.** "playwrights and translators do not directly borrow their language models from the everyday living language, but do so rather through mediation of linguistic-stylistic norms formed in literary tradition, whose influence on the… translator's style is greater than that of his own everyday language usage." She also found that source-language interference "was stronger in dialogue translation than it was in narrative texts". — [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)
- **Earlier work.** Literary dialogue is a text type "especially prone to inadequate translation". Translators flatten an utterance that is at once linguistic, poetic and pragmatic into one that is only linguistic or only poetic. — [Ben-Shahar 1994, Target 6(2):195–221](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/target.6.2.05ben) (abstract only). Her related work covers terms of address (Ben-Shahar 1987, *Bamah* 108, in Hebrew) and phonetic representation of speech (TTR 7(2), 1995), as listed in the [1998 references](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar).

**Gideon Toury (Tel Aviv)**
- *Descriptive Translation Studies and Beyond* (1995) includes:
  - Ch. 5, "Conjoint phrases (binomials) as translational solutions";
  - Ch. 7, "A lesson from indirect translation", on Hebrew literature's German period, i.e. translation into Hebrew via German;
  - Ch. 11, "Translation-specific lexical items";
  - Part Four, the "law of growing standardization" and a first step toward a "law of interference". — [Toury, DTS table of contents](https://tau.ac.il/~tarbut/Toury/works/dts.html)
- **Binomials (secondary source).** Hebrew translations contain more conjoint near-synonym pairs than original Hebrew, and more newly coined pairs. Examples come from translated children's literature, Goethe and Böll. — [secondary summary of Toury 1995, Ch. 5](https://studfile.net/preview/9130490/page:5/). I did not see Toury's own examples.
- **Growing standardization.** "textual relations obtaining in the original are often modified, sometimes to the point of being totally ignored, in favour of habitual options offered by a target repertoire." — Toury 1995 Part Four, as summarised in [Wintner's COLING 2016 tutorial](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf), which pairs it with the law of interference ("fingerprints of the source text").

**Nitsa Ben-Ari (Tel Aviv)**
- "Avoiding repetitions is one of the most recurrent norms of translation", done by "cancelling them altogether" or "replacing them with synonyms". It belongs to a norm complex that serves to "embellish", "amplify", "correct" and "clarify" texts. It reflects "a deep-rooted need to display richness of vocabulary". — [Ben-Ari 1998, Meta 43(1):68–78](https://doi.org/10.7202/002054ar)
- **On Hebrew specifically.** "Hebrew translations tend to behave just as rigidly in respect of avoiding repetitions, while favouring and inserting repertoremic repetitions. The Hebrew Enlightenment (Haskala)… was greatly in favour of collocations such as binoms and binoms of synonyms, remnants of which can be found in normative Hebrew stylistic till today." — [Ben-Ari 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002054ar)

**Itamar Even-Zohar (Tel Aviv)**
- **Void pragmatic connectives (VPCs)** are spoken-discourse organisers whose meaning has worn thin. Hebrew acquired them as it became spoken. "a larger repertoire emerg[es] in vernacular than in literary language." — [Even-Zohar 1982, ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED277227.pdf)
- **Russian VPCs.** Literary Hebrew built a set of "domesticated" Russian VPCs, "first in translated literature and gradually also in original Hebrew literature". That set "continued to be maintained in spite of the fact that spoken Hebrew… eventually created a different VPC set". In other words, written and translated Hebrew dialogue used a different set of connectors from real speech. — [Even-Zohar 1982, Theoretical Linguistics 9](https://doi.org/10.1515/thli.1982.9.1-3.11) (abstract)
- **Yiddish origins.** Spoken Hebrew's interjections and VPCs come largely from Yiddish, "either directly borrowed, like נו, or disguised, like אז". "the colloquial language was not recognized by the establishment; indeed, it is not really recognized even today." — [Even-Zohar 1993, "The Role of Russian and Yiddish in the Crystallization of Modern Hebrew"](https://www.tau.ac.il/~itamarez/works/papers/papers/EvenZohar_1993--The%20Role%20of%20Russian%20and%20Yiddish%20in%20the%20Crystallization%20of%20Modern%20Hebrew.pdf)

**Rakefet Sela-Sheffy (Tel Aviv)**
- **Data and the "custodian" self-image.** Based on about 250 profile articles, interviews and reviews from the early 1980s to 2004, one of three dominant self-images of Israeli literary translators is "the translator as a custodian of language culture". This is "a rather long-established image of Hebrew translators" that grew out of nation-building.
- **Voices of that image.** Amazia Porath, a senior translator and Academy member, speaks of "the pedant guardian [of Hebrew] that is inside me". Dykmann complains of "terror" among editors and readers against "highbrow" or "archaic" Hebrew.
- **What the field rewards.** "perfect proficiency in the canonical Hebrew language and its historical layers" is a status asset in literary translation. — [Sela-Sheffy 2008, "The Translators' Personae" (Meta)](https://www.tau.ac.il/~rakefet/papers/RS-The%20Translators_Personae.pdf); [Sela-Sheffy 2005, Target 17(1)](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/target.17.1.02sel); [HKBU talk abstract](https://ctn-hub.hkbu.edu.hk/event/the-status-struggle-of-translators-the-pursuit-of-symbolic-capital-by-a-semi-professional-group-the-case-of-literary-translators-in-israel/)

**Hebrew linguistics on register markers**
- **Lexicon drives register.** Register in texts by schoolchildren, adolescents and adults was coded as low, neutral or high. "'neutral' items constituted the bulk of the material, and… the lexicon accounted for some 80% of variation." Text types range "on a cline from everyday colloquial usage in oral narratives to more formal, high-level language in written expository essays". — [Ravid & Berman 2009, Pragmatics & Cognition 17(1)](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pc.17.1.04rav) (abstract)
- **Bound forms are optional and marked.** Hebrew has "optional bound inflectional forms such as possessive markers on nouns (e.g., armona 'her palace') and an accusative inflection on transitive verbs (e.g., re'itiv 'I-saw-him')". — [Ravid, handbook chapter](https://doritravid.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/handbook-of-orthography.pdf)
- **Colloquial Hebrew avoids them, with one exception.** Contemporary Hebrew "has no object markers on the verb in the colloquial register but does display them, to some extent, in a literary style or high register". In the oral register possession is expressed with של, "with one exception": kinship and body-part terms readily take suffixes even colloquially. — [Kirtchuk, Hebrew typology (HAL, 2011)](https://hal.science/hal-00602793/file/Hebrew_Typology_25-5-2011.pdf)

**Film, not translation**
- In 1930s Eretz-Israeli fiction films, filmmakers "adopted the normative approach to Hebrew, which strongly preferred high-register Hebrew, rather than authentically reproducing speech". — [Bar-Ziv Levy, Bar-Ilan](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/the-representation-of-spoken-hebrew-in-eretz-israeli-fiction-film-2/) (abstract via search summary)

### Inferences
- **(Mine) The bookishness in our flagged lines matches the old "rhetorical-stylistic model".** It is lexical and phraseological:
  - high lexemes: «זנחתי», «ציווה», «אסיר תודה»;
  - literal collocations: «תופעות לוואי מכוערות», «הבהלה… נוחה לנו»;
  - bent or near-miss idioms: «נושם בעורף» for נושף בעורף, «יצא לנו מזל» for שיחק לנו המזל;
  - calqued formulas and exclamations: «כצפוי מ…», «תתכונן לקראתי, עולם!».

  This fits Ravid & Berman's finding that the lexicon carries about 80% of register.
- **(Mine) An LLM reproduces Ben-Shahar's failure from the other direction.** Ben-Shahar shows translators drew their dialogue models from literary tradition, not from speech. An LLM trained mainly on written Hebrew (news, Wikipedia, literature, translated books) does the same. It knows written collocations best, so it defaults to them even when it "knows" the spoken alternative.
- **(Mine) Bent idioms are the worst-case pattern.** «נושם בעורף» and «יצא לנו מזל» are exactly Ben-Shahar's "deviated fixed expressions". A slightly wrong idiom is worse than either the exact idiom or plain words. The reader recognises the template, notices the mismatch, and stops.
- **(Mine) The synonym-variation habit applies to an LLM too.** The historical Hebrew norm of avoiding repetition and pairing synonyms (Ben-Ari, Toury, Ben-Shahar) matches a known LLM tendency toward lexical variety. Real speech repeats words.

## Q2. What research exists on Hebrew subtitling (TV, film, streaming; professional vs fansub): condensation, slang and profanity, orality markers, and does the space limit favour compact synthetic forms?

### Takeaway
Israeli TV subtitling ran under an explicit normative regime from 1968 to the early 1990s:
- elevated register, slang avoided or put in quotation marks, loanwords shunned, Academy neologisms pushed;
- all of it enforced by language editors (Nir 1984, via Weissbrod 2007).

Commercial and cable TV broke this from the 1990s. By 2007 "the norm… has almost vanished". Even under the old regime, Channel 1's own subtitlers said subtitles must be written "in the style of the living language". They explicitly told translators to **avoid bound pronominal forms (ביתו, הכרתיו)** and to keep any register lift from going above conversation level. Condensation was done by **omission and simplification**, not by switching to compact synthetic morphology. Today Netflix's Hebrew guide requires matching the original's tone, register, class and formality, with no censorship and a limit of 42 characters per line.

### Cited Findings

**The old normative regime (1968 to early 1990s)**
- **What the norm required.** "The register was often elevated compared with the original. Slang and vulgar phrases were avoided, especially in translations for children… When slang and vulgarisms did find their way into the translation, they were put in quotation marks… The use of foreign borrowed words was shunned too, even when they were more familiar to the public than their Hebrew substitutes. Thus the captions often contained newly coined Hebrew words with which the public was not acquainted. When translators deviated from the norms… their translation was corrected by language editors." The internal citation is to Nir 1984: 88–89. — [Weissbrod 2007, Linguistica Antverpiensia NS 6:23–33](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)
- **Bilingual subtitles meant one line of Hebrew.** Channel 1 subtitles were usually Hebrew plus Arabic on screen at once, so Hebrew got one line, which forced "double condensation". — [Weissbrod 2007](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177); [Kaufmann 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/003293ar)

**The shift (1990s to 2007)**
- **The norm faded.** After the 1990s rise of cable, Channel 2 and satellite TV: "Slang and vulgarisms in the original dialogue no longer pose a problem. The norm described by Nir (1984) has almost vanished." — [Weissbrod 2007](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)
- **Shakespeare films as the contrast case** (same source):
  - A 1992 Channel 1 *Othello* was "abundant with elements of elevated language such as rare grammatical forms, idioms rooted in old canonised Hebrew texts and Aramaic words and collocations". Examples: «במטותא» instead of «בבקשה» ('please'), and «צפרא טבא» instead of «בוקר טוב» ('good morning').
  - On commercial channels, "elevated language was used only when the translation was based on a pre-existing literary translation".

**Kaufmann 1998: interview with Daphna Amit, head of Channel 1 (IBA) subtitling**
- IBA "remplit une mission éducative en contribuant à la diffusion d'un hébreu correct, normatif" ('fulfils an educational mission by spreading correct, normative Hebrew').
- Its former language adviser Abba Ben-David set "des normes tendant au purisme" ('norms tending to purism'). His successor Ruth Almagor-Ramon was "plus souple" ('more flexible').
- Every channel had a language adviser, and the Academy set the norms.
- In practice: "tout dépend du type de film… la traduction tente de refléter les divers niveaux de langue de l'original" ('it all depends on the type of film… the translation tries to reflect the original's various language levels').
- Recruitment favoured people who had lived abroad, because film and TV language "est, dans la plupart des cas, une langue parlée, voire argotique, qu'on n'acquiert pas dans les universités" ('is in most cases spoken, even slangy, language that you don't acquire at university').
- The entrance test asked candidates to fix "des calques" and "un ordre des mots défectueux" ('calques' and 'faulty word order').
- Commercial channels "n'exigent pas un hébreu normatif" ('don't require normative Hebrew').
- Newspapers ran regular columns mocking subtitle blunders: *Kol Ha'ir*'s "Targumon" and pieces in *Yedioth*. — [Kaufmann 1998, "Aspects de la traduction audiovisuelle en Israël", Meta 43(1)](https://doi.org/10.7202/003293ar)
- On slang (same source): "La langue parlée évolue très rapidement et subit l'influence, notamment, de l'argot militaire. Les traducteurs réagissent subjectivement… et ils n'ont pas la même attitude devant l'argot" ('the spoken language changes very fast, influenced especially by army slang; translators react subjectively and differ in how they treat slang').

**Gazit 1997/98: a Channel 1 subtitler's working principles (most directly relevant source)**
Aya Gazit was a translator and translation editor at Channel 1. The article is based on a December 1996 lecture at the Academy's Mazia institute. — [Gazit, "ממאפייני התרגום בסרטי טלוויזיה", לשוננו לעם מט (תשנ"ח), 85–89](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf). Her points:
- **Format.** One line per subtitle, about three seconds each. Children's programmes got 5–6 seconds and "עברית קלה יותר" ('easier Hebrew').
- **Condensation by cutting.** Three seconds of speech must shrink to "ארבע או חמש מילים לכל היותר" ('four or five words at most'), and "עיקרו של התרגום מתאפיין… בקיצור ובסילוק הטפל" ('the essence of the translation is shortening and removing the incidental').
  - Example (literal translation first, then the subtitle): «מה זה? גולה! אולי זה גלגל העין! אני יודעת, זאת סוכרייה!» becomes the subtitle «זאת גולה! אולי עין! סוכרייה!» ('It's a marble! Maybe an eye! Candy!').
- **Fillers are not translated.** "אין מתרגמים מילות חלל" ('filler words are not translated'). Swear words used only as fillers are not translated either.
- **Live register.** "הכתוביות מתרגמות דו-שיח חי, ולכן עליהן להיות מנוסחות בסגנון הלשון החיה" ('subtitles translate live dialogue, so they must be phrased in the style of the living language').
- **Key passage on register drift and bound pronouns:** "עצם ההעברה מלשון דיבור ללשון כתובה והחובה לתרגם לעברית תקנית. שני אלה יוצרים בהכרח הגבהה קלה במשלב, ועל המתרגם לשים לב שהרמה לא תהיה גבוהה מדיי ולא תחרוג ממשלב השיחה… משתדלים להימנע ממבני לשון האופייניים למשלב גבוה כגון שימוש בכינויים חבורים: ביתו, הכרתיו". In English: moving from speech to writing and having to use standard Hebrew both inevitably raise the register slightly, so the translator must keep it from getting too high or leaving conversational register, and must avoid high-register structures such as bound pronouns («ביתו» 'his house', «הכרתיו» 'I knew him').
- **Markers of low register.** Slang such as «צ'ופר» ('treat/bonus') and «לפלף» ('to waffle'), and spellings that show dropped sounds.
- **Profanity** is left to the translator's judgment.
- **Normative preferences that remained.** Channel 1 preferred «נשים רבות» over «הרבה נשים» ('many women'). Subtitles were used to spread Academy coinages such as «הדמיה» ('simulation'), «משיבון» ('answering machine') and «טוגנים» ('fries').

**Today: streaming norms and audience reactions**
- **Netflix Hebrew style guide.** "42 characters per line"; "Dialogue must never be censored"; "Always match the tone of the original content… (e.g. replicate tone, register, class, formality, etc. in the target language in an equivalent way)"; for profanity, "always opt for a translation of the same/equivalent severity and intent. Avoid literal or direct translations which may result in a stronger or softer translation." — [Netflix Hebrew Timed Text Style Guide](https://studiopartner.netflix.net/studio/hebrew-timed-text-style-guide)
- **Streaming platforms now set norms.** National broadcasters first set TV subtitling norms. Now "the most influential force driving subtitling norms is arguably the global video on demand (VOD) providers". — [Pedersen 2018, JAT](https://jatjournal.org/index.php/jat/article/view/46)
- **Backlash against overshooting downward.** In the 2015 family film *Pixels*, "she is so hot" was subtitled «היא כזאת כוסית» (crude slang for an attractive woman), roughly ten times. Parents protested publicly. One parent suggested «חתיכה» ('a looker'), «מדהימה» ('stunning') or «לוהטת» ('hot'). — [Walla, 10 Aug 2015](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/cinema/2880684)
- **Censorship pulled back.** In 2021 the public broadcaster Kan stopped asterisking profanity in Hebrew captions after complaints from deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers. — [Walla, 16 Feb 2021](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/tv/3418507)

**Other existing work**
- A Bar-Ilan MA thesis on humour in the Hebrew subtitles of Almodóvar films (Bibas Shizaf 2020). — [Bar-Ilan CRIS](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/studentTheses/typology-of-humorous-occurrences-in-almdovars-films-analysis-and-)
- A Bar-Ilan MA thesis, "Differences and Similarities in Translation of Animated Films into Subtitles and Dubbing" (Stav 2011, supervised by M. Shlesinger). — [Bar-Ilan theses list](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/organisations/department-of-translation-and-interpreting-studies/studentTheses/)
- I could not access the contents of either thesis.

### Inferences
- **(Mine) The space limit does not justify synthetic forms.** I found no study showing that compact forms (חבריי, אינו, ביתו) were favoured because they are shorter. The one professional source that discusses condensation in Hebrew (Gazit) condenses by deleting fillers and redundancy and by using fragments, and in the same breath bans bound pronouns as too high. So the 44-character limit is not a valid reason to use אינו or חבריי. The historical professional answer is to cut words, not to compress morphology.
- **(Mine) Our targets have research backing.** Gazit's two-sided warning ("not too high, not beyond conversation level", while staying standard) and Netflix's "match register, class, formality" match the house target register closely. Both can be cited to the translator as norms.
- **(Mine) Fan subtitling has a different failure mode.** In the Israeli fan subtitle culture, the risk is literalism, not elevation. Kaufmann's "faulty word order" and "calques" test items are the same failures listed in our house doc's "English tells".

### Gaps
- I could not obtain Nir 1984 itself, the primary documentation of the IBA norm with examples. Kaufmann lists "Nir 1983, 1984a, 1984b, 1994", but the journal and pages are unverified. It is often cited as *International Journal of the Sociology of Language*, which I did not confirm.
- I found no corpus study of register in Hebrew subtitles: none comparing professional with fan subtitles, TV with streaming, or Hebrew subtitles with original Hebrew TV dialogue.
- I found no academic study of Hebrew anime fansubbing specifically.

## Q3. What does research say about the language of Hebrew dubbing (children's dubbing, Disney/Pixar, Pokémon) and the normative-vs-colloquial debate?

### Takeaway
Israeli dubbing has been almost entirely for children. It was historically more bound by educational and normative constraints than subtitling. By the 2000s those constraints had weakened. *Shrek*'s Hebrew dub uses Israeli slang («אחלה דיל», 'awesome deal'), with only traces of euphemism left. Scholars describe dubbing as **target-oriented**: more domesticated and more local, closer to how Israelis actually talk. Subtitling stays closer to the source. There is little accessible research on the register of Hebrew dubbing.

### Cited Findings
- **Who gets dubbing.** Dubbing was "limited to children's films and programs, at first only animated ones and later those with live actors as well". Politicians, including author and MK Moshe Shamir in 1980, failed to make dubbing compulsory. — [Weissbrod 2007](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)
- **Shrek.** Comparing the dubbed and subtitled Hebrew versions of *Shrek* (2001) shows "the previous language norm has weakened even in dubbing". Some remnants remain: "broad", said of Snow White, became «נערה» ('young woman'). Domestication is "far more conspicuous in the dubbed version": "The Muffin Man" became the Hebrew children's song «אוגה אוגה». "Freed from the previous language norm, the translator has used slang, but the slang too is markedly Israeli", e.g. «אחלה דיל». This supports Danan's view that "subtitling is a source-oriented mode of translation whereas dubbing is target-oriented". — [Weissbrod 2007](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)
- **Format choice in the 1990s.** Dubbing was used only for "programmes destinés aux enfants d'âge préscolaire, qui ne savent pas encore lire" ('programmes for preschool children who can't read yet') and costs about ten times as much as subtitling. Children's animation was broadcast dubbed on educational TV. — [Kaufmann 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/003293ar)
- **Today.** Dubbing in Israel is "almost entirely for children's films and series", with rare exceptions such as the Harry Potter films. — [Hamichlol, "דיבוב בישראל"](https://www.hamichlol.org.il/%D7%93%D7%99%D7%91%D7%95%D7%91_%D7%91%D7%99%D7%A9%D7%A8%D7%90%D7%9C) (encyclopedia, low authority)
- **Pokémon and Digimon came to Israel via the US English versions.** They were "bought from U.S. distributors after being heavily edited" and "marketed for the first time in Israel as 'Japanese'". So the best-known Hebrew anime dubs were themselves relay translations through English. — [Daliot-Bul, "Israeli Fans of Japanese Popular Culture and 'Japan'" (2013)](http://sfile.f-static.com/image/users/215554/ftp/my_files/Special%20Issue/NewFiles/daliot%20bull%20fulltext.pdf?id=15913184). A chapter "Pokémon in Israel" exists ([Lemish & Bloch, in *Pikachu's Global Adventure*, Duke UP](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780822385813-008/html)), but I did not access it.
- **Audiences notice dub dialogue that isn't funny.** One parent's Facebook post complained of having to swap "the brilliant texts Pixar wrote for the flat jokes («בדיחות הקרש») the crappy translators wrecked the film with". This is one viewer's opinion, not research. — [Walla 2015](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/cinema/2880684)

### Inferences
- **(Mine) Dubbing is the better register model for us.** For a shonen action anime, the style we want sits between today's subtitling register and the more domesticated dubbing register. "Natural" means the target-oriented choice: an Israeli idiom with the same function («הלך עלינו», «שיחק לנו המזל»), not the source-oriented paraphrase («נגמר לנו», «יצא לנו מזל»). The user's corrections all move in the dubbing direction.

### Gaps
- I found no accessible study of the register of Hebrew Disney or Pixar dubs over time, of the Hebrew Pokémon dub's language, or of the debate between normative "correct Hebrew" and colloquial Hebrew in dubbing. Popular articles praise translators (e.g. Shafrira Zakai's "eloquent dialogue") without analysis. The Stav 2011 thesis is the likeliest academic source, and I could not access it.

## Q4. Relay/pivot translation (anime via an English pivot; Netflix pivot templates): documented artifacts and mitigations

### Takeaway
Research on indirect audiovisual translation is young, mostly small case studies, and mostly about errors. Its consistent finding: overreliance on the English intermediary replicates its deviations, and English filters out sociopragmatic layers such as honorifics, speech levels and role language. For anime specifically, English subtitles already standardise *yakuwarigo* (stereotyped character speech), so any relay into a third language inherits voices that have been flattened. Documented mitigations:
- annotated pivot templates that flag register, formality, tone and intent;
- consulting the original where possible;
- **compensating** for lost character voice with target-language lexical and stylistic choices.

Indirect translation is not inherently worse. The outcome depends on the translator and on the quality of the pivot.

### Cited Findings
- **State of the research.** Indirect audiovisual translation "spans across different AVT modes (such as… fansubbing…)". Earlier studies share "the focus on the negative consequences of indirect translation, particularly on deviations from the original audiovisual product that are replicated or transferred because of the translators' overreliance on an English intermediary text". For example, Gottlieb & Grigaravičiūtė (1999), on a Lithuanian voice-over of a Danish serial made from an English pivot, found "misinterpretation and loss of information". There is "a dearth of tested hypotheses or comprehensive typologies". — [Pięta, Valdez, Menezes & Sokoli 2024, Perspectives](https://doi.org/10.1080/0907676x.2024.2374649)
- **Professionals' concerns about pivot templates** (same source): "unhelpful annotations, locked templates", and "English templates created by professionals who lack a thorough understanding of the source language or those serving dual purposes, acting both as subtitles aimed at English-language viewers and as a starting text for translators into other languages".
  - That dual purpose is exactly our situation: One Pace English subtitles are written for English viewers.
- **What a pivot template should annotate.** Netflix's template guide says annotations should explain "slang, idioms, jokes, complex grammar, sarcasm and irony, register, formality and class, tone, intent". — [Netflix Subtitle Template Timed Text Style Guide](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/219375728) (via search summary)
- **English as a filter.** Turkish subtitles of *Squid Game* made via English show that English "may have limitations in conveying the full range of sociopragmatic aspects" of Korean, acting as "a dominant cultural filter". — [Dallı 2024, JAT 7(1)](https://jatjournal.org/index.php/jat/article/view/279) (abstract)
- **A Japanese case.** A Finnish case study of *Terrace House* (Japanese, subtitled from an English template) found that pivot translation adds challenges beyond template work in general. A Japanese intralingual subtitle track served as a useful reference. — [Orava, Univ. of Turku BA thesis](https://www.utupub.fi/items/7fcc9f76-ac35-4db9-b155-63064f4acbfb/full) (abstract via search)
- **Indirect is not automatically worse.** Quoting Radó (1975): "the success or failure of ITr will depend on the talent of the translator as well as on the quality of the intermediate translation… If both are excellent, the result will be hardly distinguishable from a direct translation." Landers' "Xerox effect" warns of loss with each pass, but research shows target texts sometimes end up closer to the original than the intermediate was. — [Pięta 2014](http://hdl.handle.net/10451/27791)
- Background reviews: [Assis Rosa, Pięta & Bueno Maia 2017, Translation Studies](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14781700.2017.1285247); [Pięta, Ivaska & Gambier 2022, Target](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/target.00012.pie).
- **Character voice in anime subtitles.** English subtitling of Japanese animation faces two barriers to recreating character voice: the "major pragmatic and sociolinguistic difference between Japanese and English" and the technical constraints of subtitling. Strategies vary, but "the decimation of stylistic function is not inevitable". — [Howell, "Character voice in anime subtitles", Perspectives (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1080/09076760708669045) (abstract)
- **Yakuwarigo is standardised in English.** Yakuwarigo (old man, samurai, young lady and similar stereotyped speech, built from pronouns, copulas and sentence-final particles) "is often standardised in the target text, even though this negatively impacts the uniqueness of the characters" (citing Terada 2015). Compensation is possible "by adapting the font, punctuation and lexical choices… to the character's personality". — [Heerink 2018, Leiden thesis](http://hdl.handle.net/1887/63751)
- **Honorifics in relay fansubs (low confidence).** Korean and Japanese content reaching French audiences through English fansubs loses the tu/vous distinction, so "extreme Japanese honorifics can end up as 'tutoiement'". — [UAB MAP entry](https://mapaccess.uab.cat/node/4977). I saw this only in a search summary.
- **Hebrew fan practice is relay through English.**
  - The Israeli Pokémon fan site says "מרבית התרגומים בישראל מתבצעים על ידי תרגום פרקים ביפנית עם תרגום באנגלית" ('most translations in Israel are done by translating Japanese episodes that have English translation') and "אנחנו לא מתרגמים יפנית" ('we don't translate Japanese'). — [pokemon-il.co.il, "הסבר על התרגום"](https://www.pokemon-il.co.il/%d7%94%d7%a1%d7%91%d7%a8-%d7%a2%d7%9c-%d7%94%d7%aa%d7%a8%d7%92%d7%95%d7%9d/)
  - Hebrew subtitles of Korean dramas are often made through English. — [Zur 2018, "You Can't Write 'Pak' on Television", Telos](https://www.academia.edu/104083775/You_Cant_Write_Pak_on_Television_Language_as_Power_in_Hebrew_K_pop_Fandom). I saw only the academia.edu auto-summary, so treat this as low confidence.
- **Interference is the strongest fingerprint of translation.** Automatic-classification research finds that interference from the source language is the dominant and most robust signal of translationese, ahead of "universal" features; genre and domain can overshadow it. — [Wintner talk abstract](https://sfb1102.uni-saarland.de/session/automatic-identification-of-translationese-highlighting-the-nature-of-translation/); [Wintner COLING 2016 tutorial](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- **(Mine) Our relay setup stacks two layers of distortion.**
  - Layer 1: Japanese to English. Role language and politeness levels are standardised (Heerink, Howell), and Japanese set phrases are rendered as English stock formulas.
  - Layer 2: English to Hebrew. English interference arrives as calques, passives, word order and collocations; Wintner shows interference is the main signal of translationese.
- **(Mine) Several flagged lines look like English renderings of Japanese set phrases.** My guess, not checked against the Japanese: "As expected of…" is the usual English rendering of さすが; "Prepare yourself!" of 覚悟しろ; "How convenient" of 好都合/都合がいい; "grateful" of ありがたい. Translating the English formula word by word gives «כצפוי מ…», «תתכונן…», «נוח לנו», «אסיר תודה». The fix is to map the **function** of the set phrase to the Hebrew phrase that does the same job, not the English words.
- **(Mine) Voice has to come from the character registry, not the English line.** English subtitles are written for English viewers and flatten yakuwarigo, so the Hebrew voice of the formal old warrior, the brawler and the theatrical villain must come from the registry. Ben-Shahar shows the opposite failure in Hebrew history: elevating everyone produces "stylistic uniformity".

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures pivot artifacts in anime subtitles going from Japanese through English into any third language, and none for Hebrew. I could not access the Casas-Tost & Bustins (2021) or Oziemblewska & Szarkowska (2022) studies of pivot templates.

## Q5. Corpus studies quantifying differences between translated and original Hebrew

### Takeaway
One computational study shows that translated Hebrew can be separated automatically from original Hebrew with high accuracy, using word-level and morphological features, and that this holds across domains. I could not access its list of discriminating features. I found no corpus study of Hebrew subtitles. My own quick measurement on our data suggests the classic grammatical markers are already near the human rate. What still differs: specific formal lexemes are over-used, and spoken discourse markers are under-used.

### Cited Findings
- Avner, Ordan & Wintner (2016) "use text classification to distinguish automatically between original and translated texts in Hebrew… some of the classifiers… are, indeed, highly accurate, and scale up nicely to domains that they were not trained on… analysis of the best features provides insight into the morphological properties of translated texts." — [Avner, Ordan & Wintner, *Digital Scholarship in the Humanities* 31(1):30–54](https://doi.org/10.1093/llc/fqu047) (abstract)
- In translationese classification generally, "the interference-based classifiers are the best performing ones"; "Interference is the most robust phenomenon typifying translations". Part-of-speech trigrams (e.g. modal + be + past participle, as in "must be taken") are "an extremely cheap and efficient classifier". Related hypotheses include Blum-Kulka's explicitation (1986) and Blum-Kulka & Levenston's lexical simplification (1978/1983), both developed on English–Hebrew data. — [Wintner COLING 2016 tutorial](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf)
- About 80% of register variation in Hebrew texts is lexical. — [Ravid & Berman 2009](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pc.17.1.04rav)

**My own measurement** (not a published study; crude regexes; different episodes and arcs in the two sets). I compared the 20 human "That One Gerbil" `He.ass` files (7,773 Hebrew dialogue lines) with our 61 recent AI episodes in `scripts/onepace-translations/*.he.json` (24,853 lines). Rates are lines containing the marker per 1,000 lines:

| Marker | Human gold | AI | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| formal "indeed" (the word אכן) | 0.1 | 0.8 | AI ~8× |
| formal "therefore" (the word לכן) | 0.3 | 0.9 | AI ~3× |
| "as expected" formula (the words כצפוי / כמצופה) | 0.0 | 0.5 | AI only |
| "grateful" (the phrase אסיר תודה) | 0.0 | 0.4 | AI only |
| copula forms (the words הנני / הנך / הינו / הינה) | 0.0 | 0.2 | AI only, rare |
| negation particle series (the word אינני and its forms) | 0.1 | 0.2 | both rare |
| bound accusative verb suffix (rough regex) | 1.0 | 1.1 | about equal |
| "even" (the word אף) | 5.1 | 4.7 | about equal |
| "so/such" (the phrase כל כך) and "really" (the word ממש) | 13.3 / 8.0 | 12.5 / 8.3 | equal |
| spoken discourse markers (the words נו, יאללה, וואלה, כאילו, בקיצור, סבבה, אחי) | 10.7 | 6.5 | AI ~40% fewer |

### Inferences
- **(Mine) Grammar is no longer the problem.** In recent episodes the grammatical high-register markers the literature lists (bound pronouns, the אינני series, the relative pronoun אשר, the copula הנני) are close to the human baseline. The remaining "bookish" signal sits in:
  - a small set of over-used formal function words and formulas (אכן, לכן, כצפוי, אסיר תודה);
  - word-level collocations and idioms;
  - an under-supply of Hebrew spoken discourse markers.

  This matches Ben-Shahar (spoken markers missing, formulas calqued) and Ravid & Berman (the lexicon carries register).
- **(Mine) A per-episode detector could work like this:** compute rates of a curated "formal lexeme" list and a "spoken marker" list per 1,000 lines and flag episodes far from the human baseline. This finds candidates; it does not replace a native reader.

### Gaps
- I could not see Avner et al.'s actual top features (paywalled OUP).
- There is no published Hebrew subtitle corpus of original versus translated dialogue. Our 20 human episodes plus the AI episodes are, as far as I can tell, a larger aligned human-vs-machine Hebrew anime subtitle set than anything published.

## Actionable rules for our translator

Ranked by expected impact on the "what did I just read?" reaction. Each rule gives: the pattern, why it reads as unnatural, the natural alternative, and an example. Where the alternative is my own phrasing rather than a quoted source, I mark it as my judgment. All examples respect the 44-character line limit.

### 1. Idioms must be exact, or not idioms at all (no "near-miss" fixed expressions)
- **Pattern:** a recognisable Hebrew idiom with one word swapped, a dropped preposition, or the wrong verb. Also the wrong verb with a fixed noun.
- **Why it's unnatural:** Ben-Shahar shows "deviating from… fixed expression[s]" produces a mixed style that "does not reflect authentic speech". A reader who recognises the template stops at the mismatch. This is the strongest "what did I just read?" trigger. ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar))
- **Alternative:** the canonical idiom, word for word, including its preposition. If unsure, use plain words.
- **Examples:**
  - «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושם בעורף.» → «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושף לנו בעורף.» ('the lord of hell is already breathing down our necks'; the idiom is נושף, and it needs the לנו)
  - «יצא לנו מזל» → «שיחק לנו המזל» ('we got lucky')
- **Check:** keep a list of about 300 common Hebrew idioms. Flag lines that share two or more content words with an idiom but are not an exact match.

### 2. Japanese set phrases relayed as English formulas → the Hebrew phrase with the same function
- **Pattern:** word-for-word Hebrew for English stock formulas, which are often the English rendering of a Japanese set phrase (my judgment):
  - "As expected of X" → «כצפוי מ־X», «כמצופה מ…», «לא פחות ממה שאפשר לצפות מ…»
  - "Prepare yourself!" → «תתכונן לקראתי»
  - "How convenient" → «… נוח לנו»
  - "I'm grateful" → «אני אסיר תודה»
- **Why it's unnatural:** interference is the main fingerprint of translationese ([Wintner](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf)). Ben-Shahar shows formal-equivalence rendering of expressive and phatic elements is the clearest marker of translated dialogue. Our data: «כצפוי/כמצופה» appears 12 times in AI output and 0 times in the human gold; «אסיר תודה» appears 10 vs 0 (my measurement).
- **Alternatives** (my judgment):
  - "As expected of" → «איך לא», «לא סתם…», «מה ציפית, זה…», «ברור, זה…», «זה בדיוק…»
  - "Prepare yourself" → «תיזהר», «עוד תראה», «אני בא בשבילך»
  - "convenient" → «משחק לנו לידיים», «בדיוק מה שהיינו צריכים», «עובד לטובתנו»
  - "grateful" → «תודה», «אני מודה לך», «אני חייב לך»
- **Examples:**
  - «כצפוי מכל כוחות הצי.» → «איך לא, זה הצי.» or «לא סתם הם הצי.» ('of course — it's the Marines')
  - «לא פחות ממה שאפשר לצפות ממישהו שהפליג עם מלך הפיראטים!» → «לא סתם הפלגת עם מלך הפיראטים!» ('no wonder you sailed with the Pirate King!')
  - «הבהלה הזאת ממש נוחה לנו.» → «הבהלה הזאת משחקת לנו לידיים.» ('this panic plays right into our hands')
  - «תתכונן לקראתי, עולם!» → «עולם, תתכונן — אני בא!» ('world, get ready — here I come!')

### 3. Pick the neutral spoken word over the high or rare one
- **Pattern:** written-register words where a spoken equivalent exists, for example:
  - «ציווה» ('commanded'), «זנח» ('abandoned'), «אכן» ('indeed'), «לכן» ('therefore'), «אולם» ('however'), «טרם» ('not yet'), «כה» ('so'), «שמא» ('lest'), «הנני / הינו» (formal copula), «אינני» ('I am not')
  - «מטלטלים» used for 'shocking', «מוצא להורג» ('executed') and similar.
- **Why it's unnatural:** principle 1 of the old "rhetorical-stylistic model" is to prefer "elements possessing high stylistic value" over "common and neutral" ones ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)). The lexicon carries about 80% of register ([Ravid & Berman 2009](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/pc.17.1.04rav)). Our data: AI uses אכן 8× and לכן 3× the human rate (my measurement).
- **Alternatives:**

| High form | Spoken alternative |
|---|---|
| ציווה | אמר / ביקש / נתן פקודה |
| זנח | ויתר על / עזב |
| אכן | נכון / בדיוק / כן |
| לכן | אז |
| אולם | אבל |
| טרם | עוד לא |
| כה | כל כך |
| אינני | אני לא |
| הינו | הוא |
| אסיר תודה | מודה / תודה |
| מכוער (of side effects or a situation) | קשה / מגעיל / רע |

- **Examples:**
  - «אבא ציווה לשמור על האח של אייס בחיים!» → «אבא אמר לשמור על האח של אייס בחיים!» ('Pops said to keep Ace's brother alive!')
  - «זנחתי את התואר» → «ויתרתי על התואר» ('I gave up the title')
- **Exception:** a character whose registry voice is formal, archaic or theatrical (see rule 8).

### 4. Rebuild collocations from Hebrew, not from the English adjective
- **Pattern:** a noun paired with the dictionary translation of an English modifier or verb, e.g. «תופעות לוואי מכוערות» ('ugly side effects'), «הרגש סוחב אתכם» ('the emotion drags you'), «מחזיר ימים» ('brings back days').
- **Why it's unnatural:** this is lexical interference; translators treat words as purely referential and ignore their normal Hebrew company ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)). In the user's flagged lines, the corrections are all collocation fixes.
- **Alternative:** ask what an Israeli says about this noun, then use that verb or adjective even if its English back-translation differs.
- **Examples:**
  - «תופעות לוואי קשות» ('severe side effects')
  - «אתם נסחפים» ('you're getting carried away')
  - «איזה זיכרונות» ('what memories')
  - «הלך עלינו» ('we're done for') instead of «נגמר לנו»

### 5. Don't vary for elegance; don't add synonym pairs, embellishments or proverbs that don't sound like proverbs
- **Pattern:**
  - synonym doublets such as «מוכן ומזומן» ('ready and prepared');
  - repeated English words translated as different Hebrew words;
  - ornamental parallelism such as «גילוי רודף גילוי» ('discovery chases discovery');
  - literal proverbs such as «עכבר לכוד הוא דבר מפחיד» ('a trapped mouse is a scary thing').
- **Why it's unnatural:** avoiding repetition and pairing synonyms are entrenched Hebrew translation norms rooted in Haskala stylistics ([Ben-Ari 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002054ar); [Toury 1995 Ch. 5](https://tau.ac.il/~tarbut/Toury/works/dts.html); [Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)). Speech repeats words. A proverb only works if it sounds like a Hebrew saying; otherwise it should be plain.
- **Alternatives:** keep the speaker's repetition. Write a proverb as a Hebrew-sounding saying, or as a plain statement.
- **Examples:**
  - «עכבר לכוד הוא דבר מפחיד» → «גם עכבר בפינה יכול לנשוך» ('even a mouse in a corner can bite')
  - «גילוי רודף גילוי, וכולם מטלטלים.» → «כל גילוי מזעזע יותר מהקודם.» ('each revelation is more shocking than the last', my judgment)

### 6. Hebrew interjections, vocatives and discourse markers: replace, don't calque, and actually use them
- **Pattern:** two failures.
  - (a) Translating every English "Oh / Hey / Well / Man / You know" literally, or dressing it up («הו», «הלו», «אחא», «ובכן»).
  - (b) Too few of the Hebrew spoken markers Israelis really use: «נו», «יאללה», «וואלה», «אחי», «די», «רגע», «תקשיב», «אז», «כאילו», «בקיצור».
- **Why it's unnatural:**
  - Calqued vocatives, exclamations and connectors are "one of the major elements indicating that these plays are translated" ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)).
  - Spoken Hebrew built its own set of connectors, much of it from Yiddish, such as נו and אז, and it differs from the literary or translated set ([Even-Zohar 1982](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED277227.pdf), [1993](https://www.tau.ac.il/~itamarez/works/papers/papers/EvenZohar_1993--The%20Role%20of%20Russian%20and%20Yiddish%20in%20the%20Crystallization%20of%20Modern%20Hebrew.pdf)).
  - Professional practice drops filler words ([Gazit](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)).
  - Our data: AI uses spoken markers about 40% less than the human gold (6.5 vs 10.7 per 1,000 lines; my measurement).
- **Alternative:** drop English fillers that add nothing. Where the character would naturally use a Hebrew marker, add it, which is often where the English has a vocative or exclamation. Do this per character voice: brawlers use more, formal characters almost none.
- **Example:** "Hey, come on, let's go!" → «יאללה, זזים!» ('come on, let's move!')

### 7. Speak in fragments; don't over-complete or over-explain
- **Pattern:** turning elliptical or paratactic speech into full, explicitly linked sentences: subordinate clauses, «כיוון ש…» ('since…'), «על מנת ל…» ('in order to…'), relative clauses that spell everything out.
- **Why it's unnatural:** "complete, well-formed syntactic structures" was the fifth principle of the elevated model. It completes ellipsis, removes void connectives and replaces parataxis with hypotaxis ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)). Explicitation is a recognised feature of English-to-Hebrew translation ([Weissbrod 1992](https://translation.biu.ac.il/files/translation/shared/weissbrod_cv__publications__e_for_isf_2018.pdf); Blum-Kulka 1986, per [Wintner](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf)). Gazit's model subtitle is «זאת גולה! אולי עין! סוכרייה!» ('It's a marble! Maybe an eye! Candy!').
- **Alternative:** short clauses, asyndeton («ניסה לטלפן.», 'tried to call.'), elided subjects. The image carries the rest.

### 8. Register is characterization: elevate on purpose, per character, with authentic forms only
- **Pattern:** one uniform bookish register for everyone, or random elevated words in a street brawler's mouth.
- **Why it's unnatural:** elevating everyone erased character differences and produced "stylistic uniformity" ([Ben-Shahar 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/002059ar)). English pivots already standardise Japanese role language ([Heerink 2018](http://hdl.handle.net/1887/63751); [Howell 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/09076760708669045)). Netflix requires matching "register, class, formality" ([Netflix Hebrew TTSG](https://studiopartner.netflix.net/studio/hebrew-timed-text-style-guide)).
- **Alternative:** take each character's voice from the character registry, not from the English line.
  - Formal or old-warrior voice may use authentic high forms consistently: bound possessives on kinship and body terms («אבי» 'my father', «ידי» 'my hand'), «אינני», a measured word order.
  - Street voices use spoken markers and mild slang.
  - Theatrical villains may use flourish, but with real Hebrew rhetoric, not invented forms.
  - Never use invented, "pseudo-spoken" forms (Ben-Shahar mode 2).

### 9. Bound pronouns and other synthetic forms: off by default, never as a space-saver
- **Pattern:** «ביתו» ('his house'), «הכרתיו» ('I knew him'), «ראיתיה» ('I saw her'), «חבריי» ('my friends'), «אנשיי» ('my men'), «אינו» ('he is not'), chosen to fit the 44-character limit.
- **Why it's unnatural:** professionals explicitly avoid "כינויים חבורים: ביתו, הכרתיו" as high register ([Gazit](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)). Colloquial Hebrew puts no object suffixes on verbs and expresses possession with של, except for kinship and body terms ([Kirtchuk](https://hal.science/hal-00602793/file/Hebrew_Typology_25-5-2011.pdf)). Condensation in professional practice works by deleting, not by compressing morphology ([Gazit](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)).
- **Alternative:** «הבית שלו», «הכרתי אותו», «החברים שלי», «הוא לא». If the line is too long, cut a filler or a redundant clause.
- **Impact:** lower than rules 1–8, because our recent output is already near the human rate (my measurement).

### 10. Calibrate slang and profanity to equivalent severity, in Israeli terms
- **Pattern:** either sanitised literary insults or over-crude slang.
- **Why it's unnatural:** Netflix requires "same/equivalent severity and intent", and literal renderings that come out "stronger or softer" are to be avoided ([Netflix Hebrew TTSG](https://studiopartner.netflix.net/studio/hebrew-timed-text-style-guide)). Over-crude subtitles in a family film drew public backlash ([Walla 2015](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/cinema/2880684)). Slang should be "markedly Israeli", e.g. «אחלה דיל» ([Weissbrod 2007](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)).
- **Alternative:** for shonen, use mild, current Israeli slang matched to the speaker: «חתיכת…», «יא…», «לעזאזל», «סבבה», «וואלה», «הלך עלינו», «על הפנים». No vulgar sexual slang unless the original clearly is.

### 11. Passive "going to be + participle" and other English syntax → active Hebrew
- Already a house rule (`docs/translation-learnings.md` §1.5). The research backing: the English passive pattern ("must be taken") is a top translationese signal ([Wintner](https://coling2016.anlp.jp/doc/tutorial/slides/T5/Coling2016.pdf)), and IBA's entrance test targeted calques and "faulty word order" ([Kaufmann 1998](https://doi.org/10.7202/003293ar)).
- **Example:** «אייס הולך להיות מוצא להורג» → «הולכים להוציא את אייס להורג» ('they're going to execute Ace')

### 12. Detection across the ~340 shipped episodes
These are my proposals, built on the findings above.
- **(a) Formal-lexeme rate.** Per episode, count lines per 1,000 containing a curated list: אכן, לכן, אולם, טרם, כה, שמא, הנני/הנך/הינו/הינה, the אינני series, כצפוי, כמצופה, אסיר תודה, ציווה, זנח, «לא פחות ממה ש». Flag episodes above about 2× the human gold rate. The baseline is in the Q5 table.
- **(b) Spoken-marker deficit.** Flag episodes far below the gold rate of about 10.7 per 1,000 lines for נו, יאללה, וואלה, כאילו, בקיצור, סבבה, אחי, adjusted for scene type.
- **(c) Near-miss idioms.** Fuzzy-match each line against an idiom list (rule 1).
- **(d) English-formula map.** In the aligned `cues.json` and `he.json` pairs, flag English lines containing "as expected of", "prepare yourself", "convenient", "grateful", "going to be [VBN]", "that's all", "brings back", "ugly". These are high-precision triggers for rules 2, 4 and 11.
- **(e) Read-aloud gate.** Ben-Shahar's central point is that translators judge register by vocabulary alone and miss "syntax, rhythm and intonation". Keep the human blind-reader gate (`_NATIVE_REVIEW.txt`), and ask it to read each line aloud as the character.
