# How Israelis talk about the Hebrew of subtitles and translated TV/film

Scope: viewers, working subtitlers, critics, Ruvik Rosenthal, the Academy of the Hebrew Language. Research date 2026-11-03.
Method note: Reddit is blocked to my fetch tools, so I found no Reddit threads (see Gaps). Most of the evidence comes from Hebrew press, Rosenthal's site, Academy publications, Netflix's public style guides, Hebrew anime-fan forum threads (FXP), and a frequency list built from the OpenSubtitles Hebrew corpus.
Labels: anything marked **(my judgment)** is mine, not from a source.

## Q1. Which expressions count as "subtitle Hebrew" / translationese, and which have become normal Hebrew?

### Takeaway
A recognizable "subtitle Hebrew" exists, and it can be measured. In the Hebrew OpenSubtitles corpus, «לעזאזל.» ("damn") and «אלוהים אדירים.» ("good God") are among the most frequent whole subtitle lines, and «תודה לך.» ("thank you [to you]"), «אני מצטער.» ("I am sorry"), «ובכן...» ("well then...") and «בן זונה!» ("son of a bitch!") are very common too. Israeli critics single out the reflexive «לעזאזל» and its relatives («ארור», «מזורגג», «בן כלבה») as the clearest marks of sanitized translationese. Rosenthal and the Academy list English calques such as «זה עושה שכל» ("it makes sense", literally "it does sense"), «לקחת מקלחת» ("take a shower"), «לקחת זמן» ("take time") and «כתוצאה מ» ("as a result of") as Hebrew that "sounds bad". Some calques have fully naturalized, though (e.g. «מה נשמע», «ירח דבש»). The test is whether a native would say the phrase, not whether it came from another language.

**Profanity and euphemism (the most criticized area)**
- Walla critic Ido Yeshayahu (2024) on the series "Ted": in the first 20 minutes "fuck" is said 46 times, but the Hebrew uses the root ז.י.ן only 7 times. Even "shit" and "fuck it" become «לעזאזל». The euphemism set he names: «לעזאזל», «בן כלבה», «שתקפוץ לי» ("go jump"), «אוף». His Veep examples: «נתונים מזדיינים» ("fucking data") → «נתונים ארורים» ("damned data"); «סירה מזדיינת» → «סירה מזורגגת»; «שתזדיין» → «שתקפוץ לי»; "Motherfucker" → «בן כלבה»; "Fuck" → «אוף»; "Fuck you" → «לך לעזאזל» — [Walla, 2024-02-19](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/tv/3645014).
- The same critic calls the newer streaming habit of writing «הפאקינג פה שלך» ("the fucking mouth of yours") and «פאק נייט, פאק מחשבה ופאק פאקינג סוקרטס» "spectacular illiteracy" (עילגות מרהיבה). He wants plain Hebrew such as «פה מזדיין» — [Walla 2024](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/tv/3645014).
- Time Out (2021) mocked the Netflix Hebrew subtitles of "History of Swear Words" for writing «ז*ן» and «כ*ס» with asterisks and for needless translator notes: "you're not Channel 1, you're an internet streaming service. Calm down." — [Time Out Israel, 2021-01-07](https://timeout.co.il/%d7%aa%d7%95%d7%9c%d7%93%d7%95%d7%aa-%d7%94%d7%a7%d7%9c%d7%9c%d7%95%d7%aa-%d7%a0%d7%98%d7%a4%d7%9c%d7%99%d7%a7%d7%a1-%d7%91%d7%99%d7%a7%d7%95%d7%a8%d7%aa/).
- «לעזאזל» itself is genuine, old Hebrew. «לך לעזאזל» ("go to hell") is attested in 17th-century responsa and is the base of the Israeli «לֵך» curse family: «לך לכל הרוחות», «לך חפש את החברים שלך», «לך תמצא מי שינענע אותך», «לך תקווד», or just a scornful «לֵ---ך» — [Rosenthal, "כל מה שרצית לדעת על קללות"](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%94%D7%98%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A2%D7%99/2017/11817.aspx). So the problem is not the word. It is reaching for it automatically for every "damn/shit/fuck/hell".
- Rosenthal's "limited selection of Hebrew curses" (from his talk to subtitlers): «אֶפֶס» ("zero/loser"), «בן אֶלֶף», «בן אֶלֶף זונות», «בן זונה», «ימח שמו», «שֶׁתֵיחָנֵק!», «שֶׁתיקבר!», «שֶׁתישרף!». The English f-words absorbed into Hebrew: «מאדר פאקר», «פאק», «פאק אוף», «פאק יו». He adds that Hebrew leans on Russian and Arabic for cursing — [Rosenthal, "שדות המוקשים של כתוביות התרגום" (TransTitles conference, 2016)](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%AA-%D7%A2%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F/2015/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%91%D7%99%D7%95%D7%AA.aspx).
- «ימח שמו» is "considered slightly above the spoken register and not used much" — [Rosenthal 2017](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%94%D7%98%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A2%D7%99/2017/11817.aspx).

**God exclamations**
- In colloquial Israeli Hebrew, "instead of saying ELOHIM ADIRIM! one says 'Oh my God!', 'Jesus!', 'Christ!'" (in Hebrew letters) — [Adamczyk, "Foreign Influences on Modern Hebrew", Rocznik Orientalistyczny 2019](https://doi.org/10.24425/ro.2019.129442). Rosenthal (2026) gives «או.אם.ג'י» (OMG) as an example of global-English influence on youth speech — [Maariv interview, 2026-01-14](https://www.maariv.co.il/culture/literature/article-1272241). Yet the corpus shows «אלוהים» / «אלוהים אדירים» / «אוי, אלוהים» / «הו, אלוהים» together at about 200k subtitle lines ([CSV](https://github.com/orgtre/top-open-subtitles-sentences/blob/main/bld/top_sentences/he_top_sentences.csv)). Subtitles keep a form that speech has largely replaced.

**Calques Rosenthal lists as "Americanisms: in Hebrew this sounds bad"** — [Rosenthal 2016 talk](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%AA-%D7%A2%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F/2015/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%91%D7%99%D7%95%D7%AA.aspx)
- «זה עושה שכל» (it makes sense) · «זה מרגיש כמו...» (it feels like) · «לקחתי מקלחת» (I took a shower) · «הוא יודע איך לעשות חברים» (he knows how to make friends) · «זה הולך בלי לדבר» (it goes without saying) · «שלעולם לא יהיה אכפת לך» (never mind).
- Literal slang that fails: «איזה סרט צונן» (such a cool movie) · «אני נמעכת עליו» (I have a crush on him) · «מה אתה, אגוז?» (what are you, nuts?) · «נתקעתי בריבה» (stuck in a jam) · «עכברושים! שברתי ציפורן!» (Rats! I broke a nail) · «פצצה של הצגה» (the show was a bomb, which actually means a flop).
- Literal idioms: «הם הרימו צנים» (raised a toast) · «הוא קימט את מצחו» for "frown upon", which really means he disapproved. "I see" = «אני מבין», not «אני רואה».
- His 1999 column on "American Hebrew" says it "began in translation" but now spreads through speech and TV talk. For «זה עושה שכל» he offers «זה נשמע הגיוני» ("sounds logical") or «זה מתקבל על הדעת». For «לקחת צד» ("take sides"): «לנקוט עמדה». For «נקודה חלשה» ("weak point"): «נקודת תורפה» / «חוליה חלשה». He also names: overuse of «זה» for it/that/this (14 times on one page, 5% of the words); abstract «של» copied from "of"; and abstract action nouns as sentence subjects, because "Hebrew is at bottom a story of people doing deeds, not of actions that exist by themselves" — [Rosenthal, "ישנו האבסורד הזה של הצעירים היום", 1999](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%94%D7%98%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A2%D7%99/1999/02071999.aspx).
- In 2026 Rosenthal still calls «זה מרגיש לי» ("it feels to me") "very American, and I don't like it", but notes that some linguists accept it — [Maariv 2026](https://www.maariv.co.il/culture/literature/article-1272241).

**Calques that are now normal Hebrew (so "calque" ≠ "bad")**
- «מה נשמע?» comes from Yiddish (וואס הערט זיך); «ירח דבש» from English honeymoon; «שבור רגל» from break a leg — [Hebrew Wikipedia, תרגום שאילה](https://he.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D7%AA%D7%A8%D7%92%D7%95%D7%9D_%D7%A9%D7%90%D7%99%D7%9C%D7%94). English loan-idioms «מפי הסוס» ("from the horse's mouth") and «לעשות גלים» ("make waves") were absorbed via translation — [Kotar, "הסלנג הישראלי"](https://kotar.cet.ac.il/kotarapp/index/Page.aspx?nBookID=70165437&nPageID=70335735&nTocEntryID=70394355). «קפץ על העגלה» ("jumped on the bandwagon") is English-origin and accepted — [review of Rosenthal's 2026 book](https://www.shochatbooks.com/2026/02/blog-post_14.html).
- «נושף בעורפו» ("breathing down his neck") is itself an English calque that became the standard Hebrew idiom. The dictionary form uses the verb נושף, not נושם — [Avneon dictionary](https://www.milononline.net/%D7%A0%D7%95%D7%A9%D7%A3_%D7%91%D7%A2%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%A4%D7%95). This confirms that our line «כבר נושם בעורף» is a broken idiom.

**Politeness words**
- Rosenthal reduces Israeli politeness to four key words: «שלום», «תודה», «בבקשה», «סליחה». «סליחה» became "the queen of Israeli market-speech" — [Rosenthal, "תודה? על לא דבר!", Rav-Milim blog 2012](https://blog.ravmilim.co.il/2012/10/10/thanks/). A translator commenting under that post: "when translating from English I often dropped «בבקשה» at the start of a sentence, because in Hebrew it just sounds forced" — [same page, reader comment](https://blog.ravmilim.co.il/2012/10/10/thanks/) (a practitioner's anecdote, not Rosenthal's own words).

### Inferences
- **(my judgment)** The candidate list sorts into three groups:
  - **Avoid as reflexes:** «לעזאזל», «מה לעזאזל», «אלוהים אדירים», «ארור/מזורגג», «תודה לך», «זה עושה שכל», «בוא נעשה את זה», «קח את הזמן», «ברכותיי», «שלילי».
  - **Fine in context, but don't default to them:** «אני מצטער» (sincere apology, yes; casual "sorry", no), «אני מניח», «אחרי הכל», «זה הגיוני», «בן זונה» (real Hebrew, but strong).
  - **Neutral:** «אתה בטוח?», «בסדר», «קדימה».
- The corpus ratios suggest the uncanny effect comes from density and position: a high-register or calqued form in the spot where Israelis always say the short spoken form (למה/מדוע, בבקשה/אנא, אז/ובכן). It is not about any single word being forbidden.

### Gaps
- No sourced Israeli discussion found that names «קח את הזמן», «אחרי הכל», «אני מניח» or «האם» as subtitle-isms. The verdicts on them are my judgment plus corpus counts, and the house doc notes that the human gold reference uses «אני מניח» and «אחרי הכל».
- I couldn't get the OpenSubtitles corpus year or composition (pro vs fan share) from the repo page.

## Q2. What do Ruvik Rosenthal, the Academy and other language columnists say about subtitle Hebrew, calques and register?

### Takeaway
Both Rosenthal and the Academy treat translation, especially sentence structure copied from English, as the main channel through which "foreign Hebrew" enters the language. They offer explicit replacements. Their underlying principle fits subtitles directly: anything that isn't good in spoken Hebrew isn't good in writing either, and between two synonyms the everyday one wins. In 1997, a Channel 1 subtitle editor writing in the Academy's journal already warned that subtitles inevitably raise the register slightly and must not rise above conversational register.

### Cited Findings
- **Academy article "על ענייני ניסוח או ניסוח ענייני" (2012):** "Translation is the main obstacle in the way of correct Hebrew, and not in the translated words but in the sentence structure." Its examples, with the Hebrew fix:
  - the cleft «הייתה זו הוועדה שחננה» ("it was the committee that...") → «הוועדה היא שחננה»;
  - the dropped-ש relative «האיש אותו ראיתי» (from English "the man I saw") → «האיש שראיתי»;
  - «ובכן» / «אם כן» placed mid-sentence "under European influence";
  - «כתוצאה מ» (as a result of) pushing out «בגלל / עקב / מפני»;
  - catch-all «להתייחס» (refer);
  - «על פניו» (on the face of it) instead of «לכאורה»;
  - «הוכיח את עצמו» (proved himself);
  - the "take" family «לקחת זמן / דרך / חלק / את החוק בידיים / אמבטיה» → «חזר בו», «עשה דין לעצמו», «השתתף».
  — [hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99/)
- Same article, its register rules:
  - "Whatever is not nice and good in the spoken language is not nice in the written language either."
  - "Of two words of equal meaning, one everyday and one rare, the everyday word is preferred." «אנכי יוצא לשוח עם שקיעת החמה» ("I go forth to stroll at the setting of the sun") instead of «אני יוצא לטייל עם שקיעת השמש» is "ridiculous and pretentious".
  - Against "dignified" substitutes: «הנני» → «אני»; «באם» / «במידה ו» → «אם»; «על מנת ש» → «כדי ש».
  — [same](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99/)
- **Aya Gazit, Channel 1 translator and translation editor, "ממאפייני התרגום בסרטי טלוויזיה", Leshonenu La'am 49 (Academy, 1997–98):**
  - "Subtitles translate live dialogue, and so must be phrased in the style of the living language." Errors are most common with slang.
  - "The move from spoken to written language and the obligation to translate into standard Hebrew necessarily create a slight elevation of register, and the translator must make sure the level is not too high and does not exceed the conversational register."
  - Recommended tools: elided letters to show low register («מה 'תה חושב»), slang like «צ'ופר» and «לפלף».
  - Avoid high-register structures "such as bound pronoun suffixes: «ביתו», «הכרתיו»".
  - Don't add words for emotions the actor already conveys. Don't translate fillers. Don't translate swearing that works only as a filler.
  - The Channel 1 rule was standard Hebrew (e.g. «נשים רבות» preferred over «הרבה נשים»), and subtitles were used to spread Academy coinages (הדמיה, משיבון, טוגנים).
  — [Academy PDF](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)
- **Rosenthal's 2016 talk to subtitlers (TransTitles conference):** a catalogue of "minefields" — false friends and polysemy (fire = שורף vs מפטר; see = רואה / מבין / נפגש / בודק / מלווה), literal slang, literal idioms, Americanisms, and the "curse barrier". The full examples are in Q1 — [ruvik.co.il](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%AA-%D7%A2%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F/2015/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%91%D7%99%D7%95%D7%AA.aspx).
- **A Hebrew translators' style guide (Gengo, Hebrew edition)** gives the same register advice in imperative form: "avoid elevating the language". Prefer «ש» / «כש» over «כי» / «אשר» / «כאשר»; «מ» over «מן»; «כדי» over «בכדי» / «על מנת»; «אם» over «באם» / «במידה ו». Don't translate idioms word for word («עושה שכל»). Reread "as if it had been written in Hebrew" — [Gengo Hebrew style guide](https://gengo.com/he/translators/resources/style-guide/).
- A learner reference on Hebrew register: "A passage packed with elevated options can sound legalistic, old-fashioned, ironic, or pompous." «אנו» is "more formal, institutional, or rhetorical" than «אנחנו» — [Settemilalingue, Spoken vs Written Hebrew (2026)](https://www.settemilalingue.com/en/languages/he/grammar/spoken-vs-written-hebrew) (lower authority; consistent with the Academy).

### Inferences
- **(my judgment)** Gazit's 1997 warning describes our problem exactly: a written, normative pipeline drifts upward in register. Her list of forms to avoid (bound possessives, high-register structures) plus the Academy's "everyday synonym wins" rule gives a checkable lexicon (see Actionable rules).
- **(my judgment)** Several of our flagged lines fit these categories precisely. «זנחתי את התואר» ("I abandoned the title") and «ציווה» ("commanded") use rare synonyms where an everyday one exists. «אני אסיר תודה לך» is the "thank-you-to-you" calque, and «אייס הולך להיות מוצא להורג» ("Ace is going to be executed") copies English structure, the Academy's main complaint.

### Gaps
- I couldn't find a current (post-2016) Academy guidance page aimed specifically at subtitlers or streaming. The Academy's media guidance found is old broadcast-era material.
- Rosenthal's Maariv columns behind the paywall weren't searched one by one; only the archive at ruvik.co.il was.

## Q3. What do working Israeli subtitlers and dubbing translators say (rules of thumb, complaints, how streaming changed things)?

### Takeaway
Working subtitlers repeat one rule: never translate word for word, match English idioms to Hebrew idioms, and aim for "transparent" subtitles the viewer doesn't notice reading. Quality complaints from inside the trade are mostly about pay. Low broadcaster rates pushed work to students and produced "low-level translations with many errors". Netflix, from 2016, raised pay and published explicit norms: no censorship, match tone and register, and avoid source-language syntax. It also rejects files that are "grammatically correct" but "not natural to the viewer".

### Cited Findings
- **Yifat Shomer**, veteran house translator at Transtitle (Homeland, The Simpsons, South Park), Makor Rishon 2019, on what a subtitle is for and the commonest error:
  - "When translating a series or film, the goal is that the translation is transparent — the viewer watches the episode and doesn't even register that he is reading a translation."
  - "The most common mistake translators make is literal translation of what you hear. That's like Google Translate, and sometimes it comes out completely reversed. If there's an expression in English, you have to fit its Hebrew counterpart, not translate one-to-one."
- **Shomer, same interview, on register and working practice:**
  - She saves characters by writing «אנו» instead of «אנחנו», so a formal form is accepted for space reasons.
  - The Hebrew for a children's series differs from the Hebrew for an adult thriller.
  - New words such as «צפיית בינג'» ("binge-watching") are now usable in subtitles.
  - Her workflow: she translates mostly by ear without watching first; the text is then language-edited.
  — [Makor Rishon, 2019-01-03](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/culture/article/259707)
- **Aliza Friedman**, veteran subtitler and strike leader, Ynet 2017: "the [Israeli] broadcasters won't raise rates, so [studios] employ students and inexperienced translators, and the result is that today we find translations and transcriptions at a low level with many errors. I'm amazed the audience is willing to swallow it." Netflix paid about 10 NIS per broadcast minute vs about 5 from yes/HOT — [Ynet, 2017-01-25](https://www.ynet.co.il/articles/0,7340,L-4912524,00.html).
- Globes (2007) tied "the shabby translations on TV" to falling pay: "accuracy and double-checking stopped paying off." A union of subtitlers was being formed — [Globes, 2007-08-19](https://www.globes.co.il/news/article.aspx?did=1000244704). In the 2014 strike, about 150 subtitlers were earning about 22 NIS/hour — [התרגומיה blog, 2014](https://yaeltranslation.com/2014/03/03/subtitle-translators-strike/).
- **Netflix Hebrew Timed Text Style Guide** (binding on Hebrew Netflix subtitlers):
  - "Refrain from adhering to English/source language syntax as this can create awkward, stilted phrasing."
  - "Always match the tone of the original content… replicate tone, register, class, formality, etc. in the target language in an equivalent way."
  - "Dialogue must never be censored."
  - Profanity "of the same/equivalent severity and intent. Avoid literal or direct translations which may result in a stronger or softer translation."
  — [Netflix Partner Help Center](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)
- **Netflix on why its standards are high:**
  - "We may reject a file for containing translations that are grammatically correct, but are simply phrases and colloquialisms that are not natural to the viewer… An error like this is rarely an isolated event."
  - Its QC test: "a native… speaker with no [source-language] knowledge watch[es] a few minutes of the content. If they find examples that do not read naturally, the file is rejected."
  - The goal: viewers read "without being reminded that they are reading."
  — [Netflix Partner Help Center](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/214969868-Why-are-Netflix-s-standards-for-Subtitles-and-Closed-Captions-so-high)
- **Netflix Dubbing Creative Guidelines – Animation & Anime:** "Unless the content is a period piece or the use of language in the OV is different from the way people talk nowadays, we suggest dubbing translators utilize colloquial language when the content justifies it." Also: don't soften or censor profanity — [Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/10258372720147-Dubbing-Creative-Guidelines-Animation-Anime).
- An Israeli subtitling company's guidance shows register as a choice of wording. "What time is it?" can be:
  - polite: «התואיל בטובך לומר לי את השעה?» / «האם אני יכול לשאול אותך מה השעה?»;
  - informal: «תגיד, מה השעה?» / «מה השעה?»;
  - comic: «תגיד, יש לך שעון?».
  The page contrasts «הוא מישהו שיכול לגרום לי להיות מאושרת» (plain) with «הוא האדם אשר יכול לגרום לי לאושר» (elevated). "The translator's job is to identify the accurate one; the subtitler's art is to find the perfect one" — [Trans-That blog, 2023](https://trans-that.co.il/the-challenges-of-translating-subtitles/) (a commercial blog; moderate authority).
- **Anime-translation practice (English, directly relevant to "As expected of…" lines):** an anime translator turns the literal "just as I'd expect of a maou I chose" into "I knew I picked the right maou". This is deverbalization: work out what is really being said, then reformulate it — [Lost in Translation Hub (Substack), 2024](https://lostintranslationhub.substack.com/p/how-translation-in-anime-is-done).

### Inferences
- **(my judgment)** Netflix's "native reader without the source" QC test is what our user already does by watching each episode. It can be automated in part: a Hebrew-only LLM pass, with no English shown, that flags lines a native "would not say". Literal translation is "rarely an isolated event", so flagged episodes should be reviewed in full, not line by line.
- **(my judgment)** Shomer's «אנו» shows that practitioners accept some formal forms when they save space. Our 44-character limit is roomy enough that we don't need that trade-off for ordinary speakers.

### Gaps
- I found no interview with an Israeli **dubbing** translator about natural dialogue (for Disney, Pokémon or Netflix Hebrew dubs). Searches returned only general dubbing material and foreign-language sources.
- No Israeli podcast or YouTube talk by a subtitler was found or verified within this budget. The TransTitles conference (2016–) exists (Rosenthal spoke there), but I found no session recordings.

## Q4. What do viewers and fans say (forums, critics, social media)?

### Takeaway
Public Israeli complaints focus on three things: sanitized profanity («לעזאזל» for everything), clumsy half-English («הפאקינג»), and machine-like literalness ("looks like Google Translate"). Hebrew anime fans on FXP say natural-reading subtitles are more enjoyable. Their main complaint is that translations flatten or distort each character's distinctive way of speaking.

### Cited Findings
- Walla (2018): "If internet [fan] translators used to be inferior to the professionals, the trend seems to have reversed… many films and series have serious errors in Hebrew and in understanding the subject. ## Q5. Which formal forms are accepted as normal "subtitle register", and which feel archaic? (old vs current norms)

### Takeaway
The pre-streaming norm (Channel 1, 1970s–90s) was deliberately elevated. It avoided slang, profanity and loanwords, sometimes used Aramaic, and had language editors enforce standard forms. Commercial channels and then streaming dropped this: slang and vulgarity "no longer pose a problem", and Netflix requires matching the original register. A thin layer of compact formal words survives as accepted subtitle register, mainly for space («אנו», «כעת», «מדוע» in small numbers). Literary or biblical forms and bound possessives read as archaic.

### Cited Findings
- **Old norm:**
  - Israeli TV subtitles' "register was often elevated compared with the original. Slang and vulgar phrases were avoided… When slang and vulgarisms did find their way into the translation, they were put in quotation marks."
  - "Foreign borrowed words was shunned too… Thus the captions often contained newly coined Hebrew words with which the public was not acquainted." Language editors corrected deviations (citing Nir 1984).
  - A 1992 Channel 1 "Othello" used Aramaic «במטותא» ("please") and «צפרא טבא» ("good morning").
- **Current norm:** "The language used in the Hebrew subtitles has changed significantly. Slang and vulgarisms in the original dialogue no longer pose a problem. The norm described by Nir (1984) has almost vanished." In the commercial channels, "elevated language was used only when the translation was based on a pre-existing literary translation."
  — [Rachel Weissbrod, "Translation for Israeli television: the reflection of a hybrid identity", Linguistica Antverpiensia NS 6](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177)
- Even within the 1990s Channel 1 norm, bound possessives and object suffixes («ביתו», «הכרתיו») were singled out as too high for subtitles. Standard-Hebrew preferences like «נשים רבות» over «הרבה נשים» were still applied — [Gazit 1997](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf).
- **Accepted compact formal forms today:** «אנו» for space ([Shomer 2019](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/culture/article/259707)). In the corpus, «מדוע?», «אנא.», «אינני יודע.», «אכן.», «ובכן...» persist but are outnumbered roughly 2.5:1 to 22:1 by their spoken equivalents ([CSV](https://github.com/orgtre/top-open-subtitles-sentences/blob/main/bld/top_sentences/he_top_sentences.csv); the ratios are my arithmetic).
- **The Academy on «איני» / «אינני»:** there is no meaning difference, and both can be used in any context. That is a grammatical ruling, not a register ruling — [Academy, הבחנות בין מילים](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/category/%d7%94%d7%91%d7%97%d7%a0%d7%95%d7%aa-%d7%91%d7%99%d7%9f-%d7%9e%d7%99%d7%9c%d7%99%d7%9d/).

### Inferences
- **(my judgment)** For a shonen anime, these read as normal subtitle register:
  - «אתה» / «אנחנו» / «לא»;
  - «עכשיו» (with «כעת» only for formal speakers);
  - «למה» (with «מדוע» only for formal or old characters);
  - standard verb forms (no «'תה»);
  - mild slang («אחלה», «סבבה», «יאללה», «אין מצב», «מה נסגר») for street characters.
- **(my judgment)** These read as archaic or bookish and should be reserved for deliberate voice (an old warrior, a theatrical villain, a royal):
  - bound possessives («ספרי», «ביתו», «אבי» meaning "my father") and object suffixes («ראיתיו»);
  - «הנני», «אנכי», «הבה», «אנא», «ובכן» as an opener, «אשר», «כאשר», «על מנת», «באם»;
  - biblical verbs («ציווה», «שח», «נשא את קולו»);
  - «רודף» in the sense "follows" («גילוי רודף גילוי»).
- **(my judgment)** The register gap is relative. The same word («לכל הרוחות», «כבודו», «הוד מעלתך») is right for Whitebeard or a noble and wrong for Luffy or a Marine grunt. Rules should be per voice, not global.

### Gaps
- No viewer survey or reception study was found on which formal subtitle forms Israeli viewers accept. Section Q5's split between "accepted" and "archaic" relies on the old/new norm literature, the corpus ratios and my judgment.

## Actionable rules for our translator
Ranked by expected impact on the "what did I just read?" reaction. Format: **pattern → why it's unnatural → natural alternative(s) → example.** Sources are cited where they exist; everything else is my judgment.

1. **Copying English structure (the main problem the Academy and Netflix name).** Typical forms: passives like "is going to be + participle", "it was X who", "the man whom", and English word order.
   - Why: grammatical but obviously translated; the Academy calls structure "the main obstacle" ([Academy](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99/)); Netflix bans source syntax ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).
   - Fix: use an active voice with an impersonal plural subject («הולכים ל...»), put the subject first, and use «ש» relatives.
   - Example: «אייס הולך להיות מוצא להורג» → «הולכים להוציא את אייס להורג» ("they're going to execute Ace"); «היה זה הצי שתקף» → «הצי הוא שתקף» / «הצי תקף»; «האיש אותו חיפשנו» → «האיש שחיפשנו».

2. **"As expected of X" / "Nothing less than what you'd expect from X"** (さすが via English).
   - Why: a calque of the English formula; no Israeli says «כצפוי מ...» in a fight. English anime translators already deverbalize it ("I knew I picked the right maou") ([Substack](https://lostintranslationhub.substack.com/p/how-translation-in-anime-is-done)).
   - Fix: «לא סתם...», «רואים שזה...», «ברור, זה...», «איזה [X], אה?», «מה ציפית? זה [X]».
   - Example: «כצפוי מכל כוחות הצי.» → «מה ציפית? זה הצי.» or «רואים שזה הצי.» («לא פחות ממה שאפשר לצפות ממישהו שהפליג עם מלך הפיראטים!» → «לא סתם הוא הפליג עם מלך הפיראטים!» ("not for nothing he sailed with the Pirate King!"))

3. **Rare or biblical synonym where an everyday word exists** («ציווה», «זנח», «אסיר תודה», «רודף», «מטלטל», «כעת», «מדוע», «אנא», «הנני», «על מנת», «באם», «אשר»).
   - Why: the Academy says the everyday synonym wins and the rare one sounds "ridiculous and pretentious" ([Academy](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99/)); subtitles must not exceed conversational register ([Gazit](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)).
   - Fix: «אמר / הורה / פקודה של», «ויתר על», «מודה לך», «אחרי», «מזעזע / מטורף», «עכשיו», «למה», «בבקשה», «אני», «כדי ש», «אם», «ש».
   - Keep the high form only for a character whose voice is formal.
   - Examples: «אבא ציווה לשמור על האח של אייס בחיים!» → «אבא אמר לשמור על אח של אייס בחיים!» or «פקודה של אבא: שאח של אייס יישאר בחיים!» («גילוי רודף גילוי, וכולם מטלטלים.» → «גילוי אחרי גילוי, כל אחד מזעזע יותר.» ("revelation after revelation, each more shocking"))

4. **Reflexive «לעזאזל» / «מה לעזאזל» / «ארור» / «מזורגג» / «לכל הרוחות» for damn/hell/shit/f***.**
   - Why: the best-known subtitle-ism (118,730 «לעזאזל.» lines in the corpus); critics mock it as sanitizing ([Walla 2024](https://www.walla.co.il/entertainment/tv/3645014)); Netflix requires equal severity ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).
   - Fix — choose by function and by voice:
     - frustration: «אוף!», «חרא!», «יא אללה!», «איזה באסה»;
     - shock or disbelief: «מה?!», «מה נסגר?!», «מה זה היה?!», «אין מצב!»;
     - the "the hell" in a question: just drop it («מי אתה בכלל?!»).
   - Allow «לעזאזל» / «לכל הרוחות» for old or gruff characters. The house already uses «לכי לעזאזל, נאמי!» → «מה נסגר איתך, נאמי?!».
   - Example: "What the hell is that?!" → «מה זה הדבר הזה?!» (not «מה זה לעזאזל?!»).

5. **«אלוהים אדירים» / «אוי, אלוהים» / «הו, אלוהים» for "Oh my god" / "Good grief".**
   - Why: in speech these have been replaced (OMG and similar; [Adamczyk 2019](https://doi.org/10.24425/ro.2019.129442)), and the Japanese usually has no god in it anyway; the English "Oh my god" is a relay artifact.
   - Fix: «וואו», «וואי», «אמאל'ה», «לא ייאמן», «לא יכול להיות», «אוי ואבוי» (for older speakers).
   - Example: "Oh my god, he's still alive!" → «לא יכול להיות, הוא עדיין חי!»

6. **Broken or literal idioms.** Covers half-remembered Hebrew idioms and English idioms copied word for word.
   - Why: Rosenthal's "minefield" list ([2016](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%AA-%D7%A2%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F/2015/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%91%D7%99%D7%95%D7%AA.aspx)); a wrong verb breaks a fixed idiom.
   - Fix: check every fixed expression against a dictionary; swap in the Hebrew idiom of the same meaning; if none exists, state the meaning plainly.
   - Example: «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושם בעורף.» → «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושף לנו בעורף.» (the dictionary idiom is נושף בעורפו: [Avneon](https://www.milononline.net/%D7%A0%D7%95%D7%A9%D7%A3_%D7%91%D7%A2%D7%95%D7%A8%D7%A4%D7%95)). Also: «עכבר לכוד הוא דבר מפחיד» → «גם עכבר בפינה יכול לנשוך».

7. **English collocation calques** ("make sense", "take [time / sides / part]", "feels like", "convenient for us", "get ready for me").
   - Why: listed by Rosenthal and the Academy as Hebrew that "sounds bad" ([Rosenthal 1999](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%94%D7%98%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A2%D7%99/1999/02071999.aspx), [Academy](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%90%D7%95-%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%A1%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%A2%D7%A0%D7%99%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%99/)).
   - Fix:
     - "make sense": «זה נשמע הגיוני», «הגיוני», «הבנתי»;
     - "take your time": «אין לחץ»;
     - "take sides": «לתמוך ב...»;
     - "convenient for us": «משחק לטובתנו», «בדיוק מה שהיינו צריכים»;
     - "get ready for me": «חכה לי», «הנה אני בא».
   - Examples: «הבהלה הזאת ממש נוחה לנו.» → «הבלגן הזה רק משחק לטובתנו.» («תתכונן לקראתי, עולם!» → «עולם, תתכונן — אני בא!»)

8. **Politeness calques** («תודה לך», «תודה רבה לך», «אני מצטער» for casual "sorry", «ברכותיי», «סלח לי» for "excuse me").
   - Why: Israeli politeness runs on «תודה», «בבקשה», «סליחה» ([Rosenthal 2012](https://blog.ravmilim.co.il/2012/10/10/thanks/)); the house doc already flags «תודה לך שדאגת לי».
   - Fix: «תודה», «תודה רבה», «סליחה», «סורי», «כל הכבוד», «מזל טוב». Keep «אני מצטער» for real remorse or condolence.
   - Example: "Congratulations!" → «כל הכבוד!» (not «ברכותיי!»); "Sorry, sorry!" → «סליחה, סליחה!».

9. **Military "sir" and radio-speak** ("Yes, sir!", "Negative", "Affirmative") for the Marines.
   - Why **(my judgment)**: «כן, אדוני» is butler-speak to an Israeli ear, while Israeli soldiers say «כן, המפקד»; both forms appear in the corpus («כן, אדוני.» 40,747; «כן, המפקד.» 12,220, [CSV](https://github.com/orgtre/top-open-subtitles-sentences/blob/main/bld/top_sentences/he_top_sentences.csv)).
   - Fix: «כן, המפקד!», «לא, המפקד», «שלילי» only in radio contexts. Keep «אדון» + name for -san per the house rule.
   - Example: "Yes, sir! Vice Admiral!" → «כן, המפקד!».

10. **Insult and profanity strength mismatch** ("bastard" for kisama → «ממזר», or everything turned into «בן כלבה»).
    - Why: Netflix requires equal severity ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)); Rosenthal's inventory of real Hebrew insults ([2016](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%A4%D7%99%D7%A0%D7%AA-%D7%A2%D7%99%D7%95%D7%9F/2015/%D7%9B%D7%AA%D7%95%D7%91%D7%99%D7%95%D7%AA.aspx)).
    - Fix: pick from real Hebrew insults by voice:
      - street brawler: «יא מניאק», «חתיכת זבל», «אפס»;
      - villain: «שרץ», «חלאה», «נבלה»;
      - strongest: «בן זונה».
    - Example: "You bastard!" → «יא מנוול!» / «חתיכת חלאה!».

11. **Bound possessives and object suffixes** («אבי», «ביתו», «ראיתיו») in casual speech.
    - Why: flagged as too high even by 1990s Channel 1 ([Gazit](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/lla49-2-p.85-89.pdf)).
    - Fix: «אבא שלי», «הבית שלו», «ראיתי אותו». Keep suffixed forms for formal characters.
    - Example: «זה ביתו של אבי» → «זה הבית של אבא שלי».

12. **Abstract nouns as subject, ugly adjective calques, and overused «זה».**
    - Why: Rosenthal: Hebrew "is a story of people doing deeds" ([1999](https://www.ruvik.co.il/%D7%94%D7%98%D7%95%D7%A8-%D7%94%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%95%D7%A2%D7%99/1999/02071999.aspx)).
    - Fix: rewrite with a person as the subject and a verb; pick the Hebrew collocation.
    - Examples: «הרגש סוחב אתכם» → «אתם נסחפים» ("you're getting carried away"); «תופעות לוואי מכוערות» → «תופעות לוואי קשות» ("severe side effects").

### Detection ideas for the ~340 existing episodes (my judgment, built on the sources above)
- **Lexicon grep (first pass):**
  - curses: «לעזאזל», «ארור/ה/ים», «מזורגג», «לכל הרוחות»;
  - god exclamations: «אלוהים אדירים», «אוי, אלוהים», «הו, אלוהים»;
  - politeness: «תודה לך», «ברכותיי»;
  - high-register words: «ובכן», «אינני», «מדוע», «אנא», «הבה», «הנני», «אנכי», «כעת», «על מנת», «באם», «במידה ו», «אשר», «כאשר»;

  - biblical verbs: «ציווה», «שח »;

- **Pattern regexes:**
  - cleft «^(היה|הייתה) (זה|זו|זאת) .* ש»;
  - dropped-ש relative «ה\S+ (אותו|אותה|אותם|אותן) \S+תי»;
  - sentence-initial «האם »;
  - bound possessives on common nouns («אבי|אמי|ביתו|ביתי|ידי ») outside the formal-speaker list.
- **Per-voice allowlist:** permit «לעזאזל», «לכל הרוחות», «כעת», bound possessives etc. only for characters tagged formal/old/royal in the character registry.
