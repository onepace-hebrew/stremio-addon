# Professional Hebrew subtitling and dubbing style guides: what they say about natural dialogue

Researcher scope: what professional guidelines (Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, other vendors, Israeli regulators and practitioners, textbooks) say about register, orality, slang, profanity, correct grammar vs. speech, condensation, reading speed, line length, RTL punctuation, numbers, names, honorifics, songs and on-screen text in Hebrew subtitles and dubbing.
All sources were retrieved on 2026-11-03 unless stated otherwise. Quotes are verbatim. Hebrew in guide examples is given in logical (reading) order. Netflix's HTML shows RTL punctuation in visual order; I restored the logical order.
Notes in the text: **[mine]** marks my own judgment, not a source claim.

---

## Q1. What do Netflix's Hebrew Timed Text Style Guide (TTSG) and related Netflix documents (General Requirements, Subtitle Templates, pivot templates) say?

### Takeaway
On register, the Netflix Hebrew TTSG is thin. It has one rule against English syntax (with a four-row do/don't table), one rule to "replicate tone, register, class, formality", and one profanity rule ("same/equivalent severity"). It says nothing about colloquial vs. literary Hebrew, slang, or orality. The Netflix document most relevant to us is the pivot-template section of "Subtitle Templates". It says that when a translation goes through an English bridge, the target language must represent the original language, "not the English pivot file". That describes our Japanese → English → Hebrew situation exactly.

### Cited Findings

**Netflix Hebrew TTSG**, [partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/…/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide). Pulled through the Help Center API: page updated 2026-04-28, last changelog entry 2025-12-19.
- §3 Grammar and Syntax (added 2022-06-17): "Follow appropriate Hebrew grammar. Refrain from adhering to English/source language syntax as this can create awkward, stilted phrasing." The rule is illustrated by an image table ([attachment 7063429031699](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/article_attachments/7063429031699)). I transcribed it:
  - "Join Turbo and his posse". Don't:
    הצטרפו ל-Turbo וחבורתו
    Do:
    הצטרפו ל-Turbo ולחבורתו
    (Repeat the preposition on each coordinated noun.)
  - "Can three mermaids return him to normal". Don't:
    האם יצליחו שלושת בתולות הים להשיב אותו לקדמותו?
    Do:
    האם יצליחו שלוש בתולות ים להשיב אותו לקדמותו?
    (The numeral must agree in gender. Don't use the definite construct שלושת for an indefinite noun.)
  - "You can watch Netflix on more than 1,000 devices". Don't:
    את נטפליקס ניתן לצפות על גבי יותר מאלף מכשירים שונים
    Do:
    ניתן לצפות ב-Netflix ביותר מ-1,000 מכשירים
    (Avoid English-style fronting of the object, "on" → על גבי, and the padding word שונים, "various".)
  - "The team that designs, builds and optimizes the Netflix experience". Don't:
    …לעיצוב, יצירה ואופטימיזציה…
    Do:
    …לעיצוב, ליצירה ולאופטימיזציה…
    (Repeat ל- on every coordinated item.)
  - **[mine]** All four examples are marketing copy, so even the "Do" versions keep formal markers (האם, להשיב לקדמותו, "restore to his former state"). The table teaches grammar, not dialogue register.
- §20 Special Instructions:
  - "Dialogue must never be censored. Expletives should be rendered as faithfully as possible."
  - "Always match the tone of the original content, while remaining relevant to the target audience (e.g. replicate tone, register, class, formality, etc. in the target language in an equivalent way)."
  - "Deliberate misspellings and mispronunciations should not be reproduced in the translation unless plot pertinent."
  - "When translating swearing and profanity, always opt for a translation of the same/equivalent severity and intent. Avoid literal or direct translations which may result in a stronger or softer translation." (Added 2025-07-07.)
- §4 Spelling: "In general, please adhere to the new full script spelling rules of the Academy of the Hebrew Language… Always ensure consistent spelling throughout a single project… deviations using plene spellings such as the following may sometimes be acceptable:"
  ואו/וואו, צהריים/צוהריים, מחרת/מוחרת, הנה/הינה, אמנות/אומנות, אמא/אימא, מיד/מייד
- §17 Repetitions: "Do not translate words or phrases repeated more than once by the same speaker… Disregard if the repetition is for emphasis or has some narrative significance."
- §10 Foreign Dialogue: "Foreign words or phrases should be translated when possible (i.e. hello, goodbye, thank you, merci) or transliterated when no accurate translation exists."
- §23 Reference: The Academy of the Hebrew Language; the Even-Shoshan dictionary.
- The Hebrew TTSG has **no** section on colloquialisms, slang, or literary vs. spoken forms. Amazon's Hebrew guide has one (see Q3).

**Netflix "Timed Text Style Guide: Subtitle Templates"** (pivot section), [article 219375728](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/219375728-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Templates). Updated 2026-08-14, edited 2025-09-22.
- "Netflix does not expect subtitle templates to be verbatim."
- Condensation toolbox: "To edit for reading speed, subtitlers may follow the following strategies: deletion, reformulation, re-timing, re-segmentation, merging, employing contractions, removing established proper nouns or terms, removing unnecessary repetitions, omitting unimportant paralinguistic elements." Also: "Avoid condensing text unnecessarily and do not paraphrase."
- Required annotations for translators include "Slang · Idioms · Jokes · Complex grammar · Puns… · Sarcasm and irony · Register · Formality and class · Tone · Intent · Relationships between characters · Names or nicknames · Age".
- §33 Pivot language templates:
  - "As this is an additional layer of language transfer… it is vital that the pivot file works in the same way that a direct source → target workflow would work."
  - "When creating an English pivot file to work as a bridge between Japanese and Polish, for example, always ensure you add context and explain as much as possible about the source language file so that the Polish translation represents the Japanese in an equivalent way, not the English pivot file."
  - "The pivot language template should resemble a linguistically correct and fluent back translation which is not overly transcreated or localized."
  - "Provide a literal translation or explanation of any idioms or expressions which need to be included."
  - "Aim to keep the sentence length and structure as per the original audio."
- Pivot annotation list adds: "Formality, class · Gender · Negation and affirmation · … Mood… · Register · Tone · Intent · Relationships between characters".
- Related FAQ, [What is a subtitle template vs. a pivot language subtitle template?](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/38231759314835-What-is-a-subtitle-template-vs-a-pivot-language-subtitle-template): a pivot template "contains an English translation of the Korean source audio which acts as a template for translators… who do not speak or understand Korean."

**Netflix "Timed Text Style Guide: General Requirements"**, [article 215758617](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements). Updated 2026-07-06.
- No register rules.
- §6: "KNPs/formality tables must be created and used for translation to ensure consistency across episodes and seasons." KNP means key names and phrases.
- Line breaks: break "after punctuation marks / before conjunctions / before prepositions". Do not separate "a noun from an article · a noun from an adjective · a first name from a last name · a verb from a subject pronoun · a prepositional verb from its preposition · a verb from an auxiliary, reflexive pronoun or negation".

**Netflix dubbing principles** (cross-platform, relevant to orality):
- [Dubbing Guiding Principles](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/10258052221459-Dubbing-Guiding-Principles), 2025-09-21:
  - Excellence comes from "Natural phrasing in adapted dialogue to conform to target languages".
  - The adapter creates dialogue that "Sounds natural in the target language, both in its wording and phrasing".
  - "Please reword lines to improve sync, choosing alternate colloquial expressions, especially if this is consistent with basic meaning and intent."
- [Guiding Principles of Creative Excellence](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/11747624732947-Guiding-Principles-of-Creative-Excellence) (India-oriented, 2025-07-31):
  - "'No literal translations' is a non-negotiable requisite."
  - "Make the dub sound less like a dub… lines are spoken with ease, without being convoluted with too much passive sentence formation and literally translated expressions."

### Inferences
- **[mine]** Netflix's Hebrew subtitle rules alone would not catch our problem. Every flagged line is grammatical and keeps English-free syntax at sentence level. The relevant Netflix rules are the general ones: "replicate… register", "no literal translations", and "represent the Japanese… not the English pivot".
- **[mine]** The One Pace English subs act as an *unannotated* pivot. Netflix requires pivots to carry notes on register, formality, tone, relationships and idioms, and to keep the source's structure. So an English pivot is *designed* to read like Japanese in English ("As expected of the Marines" = Japanese *sasuga*). Without the notes, a Hebrew translator who follows the English words inherits Japanese-via-English formulas. This is the mechanism behind lines like «כצפוי מכל כוחות הצי» ("as expected of all the Navy forces").
- **[mine]** Netflix's "formality tables" are the professional analogue of our character registry. Adding a register/voice column (e.g., "formal old warrior", "street brawler") would align us with that practice.

### Gaps
- The Netflix Knowledge Hub copy of the Hebrew TTSG (studiopartner.netflix.net) is rendered with JavaScript and could not be read. I relied on the Help Center version, which is current according to its 2025-12-19 changelog.
- The Netflix "Hebrew Transliteration Guidelines" referenced in §21 were not retrieved. They concern names, not register.

## Q2. Hebrew dubbing guidelines (Netflix and others)

### Takeaway
Netflix's Hebrew dubbing guideline is the only Hebrew-language professional text I found that explicitly recommends spoken Hebrew (שפה דיבורית, "spoken/colloquial language"). The exception is period pieces, or originals whose language differs from how people talk today. It also forbids softening profanity and forbids adding obscenity.

### Cited Findings
- **Netflix, "Dubbing Creative Guidelines – Films & Series – Hebrew"**: help-center article [9786887342611](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/9786887342611-Dubbing-Creative-Guidelines-Films-Series-Hebrew), edited 2022-11-14. It links to a 7-page Hebrew PDF on Google Drive (id 1ZnM_KA1YDgUA_XuEU-MVF5mqb8OENT8N), titled «הנחיות קריאטיביות לדיבוב – ליפ-סינק לכותרי לייב אקשן» ("Creative dubbing guidelines – lip-sync for live-action titles"). Authors: Roberto Granados, Josh Jacobson, Dennis Chao. Audience: NP3 dubbing partners. Status: final. Verbatim:
  - Section «שפה דיבורית» ("Colloquial language"):
    «חשוב לנו שהקהלים שלנו "יתחברו" לתוכן שהם רואים על המסך, ושהמסר יעורר בהם את אותה תגובה שגרסת המקור אמורה לעורר. אלא אם מדובר ביצירה תקופתית, או שהשפה בגרסת המקור שונה מהאופן שבו אנשים מדברים היום, מומלץ למתרגמי הדיבוב להשתמש בשפה דיבורית כאשר התוכן מצדיק זאת.»
    Gloss: audiences should connect and react as the original intends; unless it is a period piece or the original's language differs from today's speech, dubbing translators should use colloquial language when the content justifies it.
  - Section «צנזורה» ("Censorship"):
    «…חשוב שהדיאלוג בשפת היעד (כולל מילות גידוף) יהיה נאמן ככל האפשר לגרסת המקור, מבלי להשתמש בדיאלקט או במילים שיציגו רמה של ניבול פה שאיננה משתמעת מן התוכן. אנחנו קוראים למעבדי הדיבוב לא למתן או לצנזר קללות או ניבולי פה המופיעים בגרסת המקור (תוך ציות לחוקים המקומיים).»
    Gloss: dialogue, including curses, should be as faithful as possible, without dialect or words that add obscenity the content doesn't imply; don't soften or censor curses.
  - Section «ליפ-סינק וכוונה» ("Lip-sync and intent"):
    «הכוונה המקורית של הדיאלוג נמצאת בעדיפות גבוהה»
    Gloss: the original intent of the dialogue has high priority; when perfect sync and intent conflict, it is the adapter's judgment call.
  - Section «מחקר» ("Research"): subject-specific vocabulary should be researched so the dub has "the same level of verbal expertise".
- **Netflix, "Dubbing Creative Guidelines – Animation & Anime – Hebrew"**: [article 9784898143891](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/9784898143891-Dubbing-Creative-Guidelines-Animation-Anime-Hebrew), edited 2022-11-14. It links to a Google Drive PDF that returned a sign-in page, so it could not be read. The **English** Animation & Anime version is public ([article 10258372720147](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/10258372720147-Dubbing-Creative-Guidelines-Animation-Anime), 2025-09-21) and has the same translation section:
  - "Colloquialisms – We want our audiences to relate to the content they are watching and the message to resonate with the same intention as the original voice ('OV'). Unless the content is a period piece or the use of language in the OV is different from the way people talk nowadays, we suggest dubbing translators utilize colloquial language when the content justifies it."
  - "Censorship – … Dialogue (including expletives) should be rendered as faithfully as possible, without using dialect or words that would otherwise introduce a level of obscenity not implied in the content. We strongly encourage dubbing adapters to not soften or censor any cursing or profanities used in the OV."
  - "Accents should only be recreated if pertinent to plot, character, or dialogue/humor."
- **Netflix, "Guiding Principles – Hebrew"**: [article 9761484573587](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/9761484573587-Guiding-Principles-Hebrew). The Drive PDF required sign-in and was not read. The English equivalent is quoted in Q1.
- **Dubbing research on natural dialogue.** Rocío Baños, ["Creating credible and natural-sounding dialogue in dubbing: Can it be taught?"](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750399X.2021.1880262) (*The Interpreter and Translator Trainer*, 2021):
  - Defines orality markers as "features typifying spontaneous spoken register used in prefabricated dialogue to reinforce its orality and to convey a false sense of spontaneity" (citing Baños 2014a).
  - Training aim: "To learn about the importance of reading dialogue aloud to ascertain that it sounds spontaneous."
- **"Prefabricated orality"**: film dialogue is "written text made to sound natural and spontaneous". Chaume Varela & Agost (2001) call it "prefabricated orality", as summarized in a [UAB doctoral thesis, 2014](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/tesis/2014/hdl_10803_285033/dr1de1.pdf).
- **Israeli dubbing norms (historical).** Weissbrod examined the dubbed and subtitled Hebrew versions of *Shrek* (2001) and found that "the previous language norm has weakened even in dubbing". The dubbed version used markedly Israeli slang, e.g. «אחלה דיל» ("akhla deal", great deal), while still softening one vulgarism ("broad" → נערה, "young girl"). Source: [Weissbrod, "Translation for Israeli television: the reflection of a hybrid identity", *Linguistica Antverpiensia NS* 6](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177).

### Inferences
- **[mine]** One Piece is not a period piece in the linguistic sense: the Japanese dialogue is contemporary and colloquial, with character-specific registers. By Netflix's dubbing logic, the default for our subtitles is colloquial Hebrew. Elevated register should appear only where a character's Japanese speech is itself archaic or formal.
- **[mine]** The dubbing rule "read it aloud" (Baños) is the cheapest test for our problem. A line like «תתכונן לקראתי, עולם!» ("Prepare towards me, world!") fails as soon as it is spoken.

### Gaps
- No public Israeli studio dubbing style guide was found (searches in Hebrew and English). No interview with an Israeli anime dubbing adapter about register was found.
- The Netflix Hebrew Animation & Anime PDF and the Hebrew "Guiding Principles" PDF are sign-in-gated.

## Q3. Other platforms and vendors, Israeli broadcasters and regulators, the ITA, and Israeli practitioners

### Takeaway
Amazon Prime Video's **Hebrew** guide (revised 2026-09-17) is the most explicit platform text on naturalness. It requires target-language colloquialisms and idioms, forbids unnatural literal translations, says to omit English fillers, and sets a reading-speed priority ladder. Disney's guide is vendor-only. No public Hebrew guides exist from Crunchyroll, Iyuno, Deluxe, ZOO or TransPerfect, as far as I found. Israeli broadcasters publish nothing on register. The Second Authority's rules require only «עברית רהוטה» ("fluent Hebrew"). The most concrete Hebrew register rules come from practitioner and teaching material: a Qsubs translator guide and a Beit Berl subtitling lecture.

### Cited Findings

**Amazon Prime Video, "Hebrew (Israel) Localization Style and Technical Guide"** ("Last Revision: September 17, 2026"). It is a file inside [AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/CooperWebsite/dvp/downloads/AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip), linked from [Prime Video Video Central → Languages, localization, and genres](https://videocentral.amazon.com/support/delivery-experience/mec-title-meta-data/languages-and-genres).
- General Subtitling Rules:
  - "Subtitle translation, creation, or quality check must always be done alongside watching the video."
  - "Be concise while maintaining faithfully the meaning of the source-language subtitle. Translate the main points and avoid superfluous or redundant text."
  - "As needed for readability, condense, substitute, or paraphrase long and complex phrases with shorter and simpler ones."
  - "Literal translations do not always accurately convey the intended meaning, cultural nuances, or idiomatic expressions of the original content. Use established or standard target-language expressions, especially for idioms, metaphors, colloquialisms, proverbs. For puns and wordplays, try to create a pun with a similar effect."
  - "Filler words, like 'you know,' 'well,' 'okay,' and 'like' are frequently used in English, as is the habit of beginning sentences with 'And.' When these words are used as fillers, it is better to omit them."
- §20 Colloquialism:
  - "Translate the intended meaning of source language colloquialisms; avoid unnatural literal translations, except when such unnaturalness is deliberate in the original dialogue."
  - "Use colloquialisms and idioms in the target language that are culturally specific and contextually appropriate."
  - "Do not use regional slangs or idioms that would not be widely understood."
- §21 Profanity:
  - "Communicate the equivalent level of profanity in the context of the Jewish culture. Convey the essence of the source content without censoring or toning down. Do not increase the level of perceived obscenity. Provide ease of comprehension."
  - "Depending on the culture, it may be recommended to tone down profanity…"
  - "In cases where the expletive is bleeped… subtitle the first letter of the expletive and represent the rest of the expletive with ellipses."
- §9 Timing priority, when 17 cps cannot be met: (1) extend the out-time by up to 0.5 s; (2) "Condense the subtitle without altering or losing the intended meaning of the audio"; (3) merge or split events; (4) "Let the reading speed go up to 21 characters per second."
- §13 Spelling: full script (ktiv male), with listed allowed variants:
  צוהריים/צהריים, מיד/מייד, אימא/אמא, מחרת/מוחרת, הנה/הינה, אמנות/אומנות
  Add vowel letters to avoid ambiguity, e.g.:
  מנהל/מינהל, תכנה/תוכנה, חכמה/חוכמה
- §12 Names: "Character Names should always be transliterated unless the content is for children." Nicknames: "Only translate if they convey a specific meaning or if they are well-known and have recognized Hebrew equivalents." For "historical/mythical/cartoon character names – Use established, or well-known Hebrew-specific translations." Fantasy places: "transliterated unless the name has a specific meaning."
- §16: "Do not use italics in Hebrew subtitles."

**Disney+**: the "Disney Digital Supply Chain Subtitle and CC Style Guide" (2022) is listed by [ESIST](https://esist.org/resources/avt-guidelines-and-policies/) at an S3 URL, but that URL returned HTTP 403 on 2026-11-03.   «בעל זיכיון ידאג לתרגום תקני לעברית רהוטה של כל התכניות שאינן בעברית, באמצעות כתוביות תרגום או באמצעות דיבוב»

**Israeli practitioner and teaching material** (the most concrete Hebrew register rules found):
- **Qsubs, «מדריך למתרגמים» ("Translators' guide")**: a training guide for new subtitlers on a large Israeli fansub site, by user "Godfather", first posted 2010-08-30 ([qsubs.net](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907)). It is a community guide, not an industry standard, and dated (a 32-character line limit). Verbatim:
  - **«משלב לשוני»** ("Linguistic register"):
    «יש להתאים את גובה השפה אל המקור ואל הדובר. יש לא מעט מתרגמים שכל הדמויות שהם אי פעם יתרגמו ידברו בדיוק אותו הדבר, וזו טעות. ילדים בני 12, חברי כנופייה או בורים כפריים לא ישתמשו במילים "היכן", "מדוע", "האם" או "מנין לך", וכך גם רובנו בחיי היום יום. אין סיבה להעניק להם שפה גבוהה יותר שלא לצורך. ומאידך, אם אתם מתרגמים סרט שמתרחש בתקופות קדומות יותר… אל תתביישו להעלות את המשלב… השתדלו לא להפוך את התרגום ל"ישראלי מדי", ולהימנע ממילות סלנג ישראליות מדי כמו "סבבה" או "אשכרה"… בסופו של דבר, המשפט שאתם כותבים צריך להישמע אמין מפי הדמות הדוברת.»
    Gloss: match the height of language to the source and the speaker. Many translators make every character speak the same, which is a mistake. 12-year-olds, gang members or rural boors won't say היכן ("where", bookish), מדוע ("why", bookish), האם (the question particle) or מנין לך ("whence do you know"), and neither do most of us day to day. Raise the register only for older periods. Don't make it "too Israeli" with slang like סבבה ("cool/OK") or אשכרה ("for real"). The line must sound credible from that character's mouth.
  - **«ניסוח»** ("Phrasing"):
    «כל משפט שאתם כותבים צריך להישמע אמין בשפה העברית. ביטויים וסלנגים באנגלית יש לתרגם לביטויים וסלנגים מקבילים (אם יש) בעברית ולא לתרגם כפי שהם.»
    Gloss: every sentence must sound credible in Hebrew; translate English idioms and slang with parallel Hebrew idioms and slang if they exist, not as-is.
    Examples given:
    - "Third time's a charm" → «פעם שלישית גלידה» (the Hebrew idiom), not «פעם שלישית קסומה» (literal "third time magical").
    - "I could use some holiday" → «לא תזיק לי חופשה» ("a holiday wouldn't hurt me"), not «אני יכול להשתמש בחופשה» (literal "I can use a holiday").
    - "I bet she has a cigarette" → «אני בטוח שיש לה סיגריה» ("I'm sure she has one"), not «אני מתערב» (literal "I wager").
  - Subject pronouns:
    «טעות נפוצה נוספת היא שימוש במילה "אני" או "אנחנו" בתחילת משפט פועל בעבר, שזה מיותר לחלוטין.»
    Example: «ראיתי סרט» not «אני ראיתי סרט» ("I saw a film"). Keep the pronoun only for contrastive emphasis.
  - No sanitizing:
    «כשדמות אומרת "we fucked" אין שום סיבה לייפות את זה ל"קיימנו יחסי מין"… זה עובד גם בכיוון השני»
    Gloss: don't prettify ("we had intercourse"); this works in the other direction too (don't coarsen "penis").
  - **«הגהה יבשה»** ("Dry proofread"):
    «קריאת הטקסט ללא הווידאו… הזדמנות מצוינת לאתר את הניסוחים הבעייתיים ואת האנגלוזים (תרגום מילולי מדי) שלא נשמעים טוב בעברית»
    Gloss: read the Hebrew without the video or the English; the best way to catch problematic phrasing and "Anglicisms" (overly literal translation).
  - Plain words over dictionary words:
    «המילים "חמרמורת" או "חברת הזנק" אולי מתאימות לאנציקלופדיה, אבל… בתרגום טוב יהיה כתוב "האנגאובר" ו"חברת סטארט אפ", כדברי פשוטי העם.»
    Gloss: Academy-style coinages like חמרמורת ("hangover") or חברת הזנק ("start-up company") suit an encyclopedia; a good translation says האנגאובר and חברת סטארט אפ, as ordinary people do.
- **Beit Berl College lecture slides, «תרגום כתוביות – אילוצים ייחודיים וכלים שימושיים»** ("Subtitling – unique constraints and useful tools") ([SlideShare 13110774](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/ss-13110774/13110774); undated, about 2012 judging by the ID and the channels cited). By a working Israeli TV subtitler who says she relies on the ESIST code. Verbatim:
  - Register:
    «התאמת המשלב בתרגום לפי הדיבור… [לעברית בכתוביות יש לפעמים נטייה להפוך גבוהה לפעמים – במיוחד כאשר רוצים לחסוך מקום יקר ולכתוב "מכוניתו" במקום "המכונית שלו"]»
    Gloss: subtitle Hebrew tends to go high, especially to save precious space: "his-car" (one suffixed word) instead of "the car of his".
  - Common errors:
    «טעויות נפוצות – הנמכה כשלא צריך (למשל בקריינות), והגבהה כשלא צריך (שפה מדוברת, סלנג)»
    Gloss: lowering when not needed (e.g., narration) and raising when not needed (spoken language, slang).
  - Space-saving tools she lists:
    - shortened forms «מהו, מתישהו, אינני» ("what-is-it", "sometime", "I-am-not");
    - pragmatic omissions (exclamations, vocatives, hedges);
    - suffix possessives «שמותיהם, בניי, ספרי» ("their-names", "my-sons", "my-books");
    - pronoun-less verbs «אשמור, תלך, נאכל» ("I'll keep", "you'll go", "we'll eat").
    With a caveat on possessives:
    «שייכות: הבית שלו / ביתו – לא תמיד נשתמש באופציה הזאת אם המשלב לא מאפשר»
    Gloss: "his house" (analytic) vs. "his-house" (suffix); we won't always use the suffix if the register doesn't allow it.
  - Grammar vs. speech:
    «על השפה להיות נכונה (דקדוקית) כיוון שכתוביות משמשות כמודל לאוריינות»
    Gloss: the language must be grammatically correct because subtitles model literacy. This is the ESIST rule 9 (see Q4).
  - Line length: «28-32 תווים כולל רווחים לטלוויזיה, ועד 35 תווים אם זה באינטרנט» (28–32 characters including spaces for TV, up to 35 online). Bottom line longer.
- **Interview with the in-house subtitler at Transtitles**, one of Israel's leading subtitle houses ([Makor Rishon, 2019-01-03, «חיה בסדרה»](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/culture/article/259707)):
  - «הטעות הכי נפוצה של מתרגמים היא תרגום מילולי ישירות ממה שאתה שומע… אם יש ביטוי באנגלית, צריך להתאים לו את המקבילה שלו בעברית ולא לתרגם אחד לאחד.»
    Gloss: the most common error is literal translation; find the Hebrew equivalent of an English idiom, not one-to-one.
  - On space:
    «במקום לכתוב 'אנחנו' כותבים 'אנו', וככה חוסכים בתווים»
    Gloss: instead of אנחנו ("we") we write אנו ("we", literary), and so save characters.
  - Workflow: texts go through «עריכה לשונית» (language editing) after translation.

**Other general guides with clear register statements** (not Hebrew-specific):

Avoid rewriting dialogue into polished or improved English and normalising dialect, social register or emotional bluntness."

### Inferences
- **[mine]** Two Israeli practitioner sources (Beit Berl and Transtitles) say outright that Israeli subtitle Hebrew drifts upward partly *to save space*: אנו, מכוניתו, אינני. With our 44-character budget, which is more generous than 28–35 or 42, there is rarely a space excuse.
- **[mine]** The Qsubs register paragraph is the closest thing to a ready-made Hebrew house rule:

  It fits our target exactly: colloquial, character-specific, not slangy for its own sake.

## Q4. Textbook and research guidance on orality and register in subtitling, and the Hebrew-specific history

### Takeaway
The canonical code (Ivarsson & Carroll / ESIST) contains a known contradiction between "register must correspond with the spoken word" and "language should be grammatically correct". Díaz Cintas & Remael resolve it this way:
- clean up grammar;
- keep interactional features;
- keep characters' distinct speech where it matters.

Pedersen's FAR model gives a usable error taxonomy: idiomaticity errors are "nativelike selection of expression" failures, mostly caused by source interference. Hebrew-specific research shows that elevated register is an old institutional norm of Israeli subtitling and translated dialogue. It has "almost vanished" in commercial practice, and translated Hebrew dialogue historically took a hybrid "pseudo-spoken" form. That form closely matches the symptom we're seeing.

### Cited Findings
- **Ivarsson & Carroll, *Code of Good Subtitling Practice* (1998, endorsed by ESIST)** ([ESIST resources page](https://esist.org/resources/avt-guidelines-and-policies/); [text copy](https://enlalunadebabel.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/reglas_subtitulacic3b3n.pdf)):
  - "3) Translation quality must be high with due consideration of all idiomatic and cultural nuances."
  - "5) Where compression of dialogue is necessary, the results must be coherent."
  - "8) The language register must be appropriate and correspond with the spoken word."
  - "9) The language should be (grammatically) 'correct' since subtitles serve as a model for literacy."
  - "13) Obvious repetition of names and common comprehensible phrases need not always be subtitled."
- **Díaz Cintas & Remael, *Audiovisual Translation: Subtitling*.** Excerpts hosted on ebrary. They cite 2010–2012 works, so I take them to be from the 2nd edition (Routledge 2021) **[mine: edition inferred]**.
  - ["Speech to writing: a matter of compromise"](https://ebrary.net/282565/language_literature/speech_writing_matter_compromise): "traditional commercial subtitling has developed a style of its own that has an impact on grammar and register, as well as on the interactional and other oral features of dialogue. It moves speech a step closer to the more careful organization of writing… Grammar, syntax and lexical items tend to be simplified and cleaned up, whereas interactional features and intonation are only maintained to some extent (e.g. through word order, rhetorical questions, occasional interjections and incomplete sentences)… Many of these traits can be salvaged in writing, but rendering them all would lead to illegible and exceedingly long subtitles… context-renewing clauses are retained, whereas context-confirming ones are dropped."
  - ["Conflicting priorities, difficult decisions"](https://ebrary.net/282604/language_literature/conflicting_priorities_difficult_decisions): "AVT, and commercial interlingual subtitles especially, still displays a preference for standard language, neutral word order and simple well-formed sentences… often limiting the type and amount of marked and improper language." On Code rules 8 and 9: "'Correct' can be interpreted in many ways, and a linguistic register that corresponds with 'the spoken word' is not necessarily incorrect." And: "If all characters speak the same linguistic variant, it can be argued that the use of standard register in the subtitles does not betray any of the characters; however, if one or a few stand out because of the type of language they speak, this should somehow be reflected in the dialogue exchanges." The same page cites Kaufmann (2004) on immigrant Hebrew homogenized into standard French subtitles, where part of the "informative/denotative meaning of the film was thus lost".
- **Pedersen, "The FAR model: assessing quality in interlingual subtitling"** (*JoSTrans* 28, July 2017, [doi:10.26034/cm.jostrans.2017.239](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1)):
  - "Stylistic errors… Examples of stylistic errors would be erroneous terms of address, using the wrong register (too high or too low) or any other use of language that is out of tune with the style of the original."
  - Acceptability errors "are those that make the subtitles sound foreign or otherwise unnatural… 1) grammar errors 2) spelling errors, 3) errors of idiomaticity."
  - "idiomaticity is not meant to signify only the use of idioms, but the natural use of language; i.e. that which would sound natural to a native speaker… 'nativelike selection of expression in a given context'… Errors that fall into this category are not grammar errors, but errors which sound unnatural in the target language. The main cause of these error is source text interference, so that the result is 'translationese'… Also, garden-path sentences may be penalised under this heading, as they cause regressions (re-reading)."
  - On grammar: "Subtitling can be seen as hybrid form of spoken and written language… a strict application of written language grammar rules may be misguided. Many languages… allow for certain typical spoken-language features in subtitling… e.g. certain cases of subject deletion, incomplete sentences and shortened forms of pronouns."
  - Scoring: penalty points per error, divided by the number of subtitles, giving separate F, A and R scores.
- **Hebrew subtitling norm history.** Weissbrod, ["Translation for Israeli television: the reflection of a hybrid identity"](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177) (*Linguistica Antverpiensia NS* 6; written around 2007; the DOI record is dated 2021), summarizing Nir (1984):
  - "The register was often elevated compared with the original. Slang and vulgar phrases were avoided… When slang and vulgarisms did find their way into the translation, they were put in quotation marks… The use of foreign borrowed words was shunned too, even when they were more familiar to the public than their Hebrew substitutes… their translation was corrected by language editors."
  - Then: "The language used in the Hebrew subtitles has changed significantly. Slang and vulgarisms in the original dialogue no longer pose a problem. The norm described by Nir (1984) has almost vanished."
  - A 1992 Channel 1 *Othello* was "abundant with elements of elevated language such as rare grammatical forms, idioms rooted in old canonised Hebrew texts and Aramaic words", e.g. «במטותא» (Aramaic "please") rather than «בבקשה», and «צפרא טבא» (Aramaic "good morning") rather than «בוקר טוב». Commercial channels used elevated language "only when the translation was based on a pre-existing literary translation".
- **Translated Hebrew dialogue as a "mixed" register.** Rina Ben-Shahar, ["The Phonetic Representation of Spoken Language in Modern Hebrew Literature"](https://doi.org/10.7202/037226ar):
  - 1940s–60s writers "wrote artificial dialogues in mixed language, mainly modelled on standard and super-standard written language norms, in which certain spoken Hebrew elements were embedded."
  - Translated fiction of the 1970s–80s "still employs a relatively conservative language, and its dialogues are still often written in the mixed language".
  - "The language of translations into Hebrew was, to a great extent, artificial. It was heavily loaded, learned, abounding with collocations borrowed from ancient written sources and rare words."
  - See also Ben-Shahar, ["Translating Literary Dialogue: A Problem and Its Implications for Translation into Hebrew"](https://benjamins.com/catalog/target.6.2.05ben) (*Target* 6:2, 1994): translators "are likely to reduce a linguistic-poetic-pragmatic source utterance into a linguistic or linguistic-poetic one".
  - An auto-summary of her play-translation study on [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/55318374/The_Language_of_Plays_Translated_into_Hebrew_from_English_and_French_A_Cultural_Stylistic_Study) names three modes: "standard Hebrew, pseudo-spoken, and authentic spoken Hebrew". Treat it as secondary: it is a site-generated summary.
- **Early Hebrew film dialogue.** Miri Bar-Ziv Levy (*Journal of Jewish Studies*, 2021, [link](https://liverpooluniversitypress.co.uk/doi/10.18647/3486/jjs-2021)): 1930s filmmakers "adopted the normative approach to Hebrew, which strongly preferred high-register Hebrew over authentic usage".
- **Academy of the Hebrew Language on negation register.** Ronit Gadish, «עניין של סגנון» ("A matter of style") ([hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/sheelot_teshuvot/mivharteshuvot/inyan-shel-signon/)):
  «ניסוחים כגון 'אני לא מבין', 'את לא יודעת'… טבעיים לגמרי לדוברי העברית בימינו. אך מתקני הלשון ממליצים להימנע מהם בלשון הכתובה ובדיבור בנסיבות רשמיות, ולנקוט 'אינני מבין', 'אינך יודעת'»
  Gloss: «אני לא מבין» ("I don't understand", with לא) is completely natural to today's speakers; purists recommend «אינני מבין» only in writing and formal speech.

### Inferences
- **[mine]** The user's complaint fits the "pseudo-spoken" or "mixed" register that Ben-Shahar describes. The sentences are spoken in structure but carry written or high lexis (ציווה "commanded", לקראת "towards", כצפוי "as expected", גילוי רודף גילוי "discovery chases discovery"). ## Q5. Does any guide address the trade-off between space pressure (compact literary forms) and natural spoken forms?

### Takeaway
No platform guide (Netflix, Amazon, ESIST) addresses it explicitly. Israeli practitioner sources do:
- Beit Berl names the suffix possessive («מכוניתו» "his-car" vs. «המכונית שלו» "the car of his») as a space-driven upward drift and says not to use it when the register doesn't allow it.
- The Transtitles subtitler describes writing אנו for אנחנו (both "we"; אנו is literary) to save characters.
- Qsubs bans bookish question words for ordinary speakers regardless of space.

The professional condensation toolkits (Netflix, Amazon, Díaz Cintas & Remael, Pedersen) all prefer deletion, reformulation and spoken-language shortcuts over raising the register.

### Cited Findings
- Beit Berl: «לעברית בכתוביות יש לפעמים נטייה להפוך גבוהה… במיוחד כאשר רוצים לחסוך מקום יקר ולכתוב "מכוניתו" במקום "המכונית שלו"» (subtitle Hebrew sometimes tends to go high, especially to save precious space); «שייכות: הבית שלו / ביתו – לא תמיד נשתמש באופציה הזאת אם המשלב לא מאפשר» (possession: "his house"/"his-house" — we won't always use the suffix if the register doesn't allow it) — [SlideShare](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/ss-13110774/13110774).
- Transtitles house subtitler: «במקום לכתוב 'אנחנו' כותבים 'אנו', וככה חוסכים בתווים» (instead of אנחנו we write אנו and save characters) — [Makor Rishon 2019](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/culture/article/259707).
- Qsubs: speakers in everyday registers don't say «היכן, מדוע, האם, מנין לך» ("where, why, [question particle], whence"); «אין סיבה להעניק להם שפה גבוהה יותר שלא לצורך» (no reason to give them higher language unnecessarily) — [Qsubs guide](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907).
- Netflix template condensation strategies: "deletion, reformulation, re-timing, re-segmentation, merging, employing contractions, removing established proper nouns or terms, removing unnecessary repetitions, omitting unimportant paralinguistic elements" — [Netflix Subtitle Templates](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/219375728-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Templates).
- Amazon Hebrew: omit English fillers; "condense, substitute, or paraphrase long and complex phrases with shorter and simpler ones"; the timing ladder allows up to 21 cps before meaning is cut — [Amazon Hebrew guide, Sept 2026](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/CooperWebsite/dvp/downloads/AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip).
- Díaz Cintas & Remael 2007 (pp. 163–166) suggest omission at word level of "tags, modifiers, adjectives and adverbs, phatic words, greetings, interjections, vocatives, courtesy formulas, hesitations and false starts", as summarized in the [UAB thesis](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/tesis/2014/hdl_10803_285033/dr1de1.pdf). Context-confirming clauses are dropped first ([ebrary](https://ebrary.net/282565/language_literature/speech_writing_matter_compromise)).
- Pedersen: spoken features acceptable in subtitles include "subject deletion, incomplete sentences and shortened forms of pronouns" — [FAR model](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1).
- Academy (Gadish): «לא מבין» ("not understand", with לא) is natural speech; «אינני מבין» (formal negation) is for writing and formal speech — [hebrew-academy.org.il](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/sheelot_teshuvot/mivharteshuvot/inyan-shel-signon/).

### Inferences
- **[mine]** The literary forms save very little:
  - «אנו» vs. «אנחנו» ("we"): 3 characters.
  - «ספינתנו» vs. «הספינה שלנו» ("our ship"): 5.
  - «אינני» vs. «אני לא» ("I'm not"): 1.
  - «כעת» vs. «עכשיו» ("now"): 2.
  - «היכן» vs. «איפה» ("where"): 0.
  - «מדוע» vs. «למה» ("why"): the literary form is *longer* by 1.

  Many bookish choices are therefore not space-driven at all. They are default-register choices.
- **[mine]** Spoken Hebrew has its own short forms that keep register intact:
  - dropping the subject pronoun on past and future verbs: «ראיתי» ("I saw"), «נלך» ("we'll go");
  - dropping «האם», since a question mark suffices;
  - «כי» for «מכיוון ש» ("because");
  - «אם» for «במידה ו» ("if");
  - «גם» for «כמו כן» ("also");
  - dropping vocatives and fillers;
  - quantifier suffixes that are fully colloquial: «שנינו, שניכם, כולנו» ("both of us", "both of you", "all of us");
  - prepositional suffixes, which are always colloquial: «שלי, איתך, בשבילו, ממני» ("mine", "with you", "for him", "from me").

  Some spoken forms are *longer*: «ככה» vs. «כך» ("like this/so"), «של» constructions. Here the budget should be spent on register, and content trimmed instead.
- **[mine]** Kinship nouns with a suffix carry a special trap:
  - «אבי» / «אמי» ("my father" / "my mother") are bookish; «אבא שלי» / «אמא שלי» are natural.
  - «אחי» in modern speech mostly means "bro". So "my brother" should usually be «אח שלי» or «אחי הקטן» ("my little brother").

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how often space pressure actually forces a literary form in Hebrew subtitles. A corpus count on our own 340 episodes could answer this.

## Q6. Mechanical conventions (reading speed, line length, RTL punctuation, numbers, names, foreign words, honorifics, songs, on-screen text) compared with house conventions

### Takeaway
The mechanical rules are broadly consistent across Netflix and Amazon Hebrew: 42 characters per line, 2 lines, 17 cps for adults, no italics, U+2026 ellipsis, a second-speaker hyphen with no space, numbers 1–10 in words. Our house values (≤44 characters, no italics, a leading RTL mark) sit within professional norms. None of the guides addresses Japanese honorifics into Hebrew.

### Cited Findings
- **Line length and lines.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "42 characters per line"; "Maximum two lines"; "Text should usually be kept to one line, unless it exceeds the character limitation"; "Prefer a bottom-heavy pyramid shape… but avoid having just one or two words on the top line" ([Netflix Hebrew TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).
  - Amazon Hebrew: "No more than 2 lines. No more than 42 characters per line… try to create bottom heavy, pyramid-like subtitles" ([Amazon zip](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/CooperWebsite/dvp/downloads/AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip)).
  - Older Israeli TV norm: 28–32 characters (Beit Berl); Qsubs (2010): 32.
- **Reading speed.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "Adult programs: Up to 17 characters per second. Children's programs: Up to 13". SDH up to 20 and 17.
  - Amazon Hebrew: 17 and 13, with a last resort of "up to 21 characters per second".
  - Minimum duration 5/6 s; maximum 7 s (Netflix General Requirements; Amazon).
- **Ellipses and continuity.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "use the single smart character (U+2026)"; "Do not use an ellipsis when a sentence is split between two continuous subtitles"; use it for a pause of 2 s or more, or an abrupt interruption. Example:
    …עמדתי להגיד לך ש
    -אני לא רוצה לדעת מזה!
    ("I was about to tell you that…" / "I don't want to know about it!")
  - Amazon: the same, plus a trailing-off example.
- **Dual speakers.** Netflix Hebrew: "use a hyphen without a space to denote the second speaker only, with a maximum of one character per line… Text in each line… must be a contained sentence." Amazon: the same.
- **Punctuation.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "Both Hebrew and Latin punctuation marks are accepted"; the interrobang ?!/!? is allowed "when a character exhibits great surprise or shock"; "Do not overuse exclamation marks."
  - Quotes: "Use double quotation marks… Use single quotation marks… for quotes within quotes"; a multi-subtitle quote opens with " on each subtitle and closes only at the end.
- **Italics.** Netflix Hebrew §11: "Do not use italics." Amazon Hebrew §16: "Do not use italics in Hebrew subtitles." This matches the house rule.
- **RTL technical.**
  - Netflix: RTL TTML must declare direction; EBU STL is not accepted for RTL ([Netflix RTL FAQ](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/236229167-What-are-Netflix-s-requirements-for-right-to-left-RTL-language-timed-text)).
  - Qsubs (SRT era): a number at line start displays at line end unless an invisible RTL mark is inserted first, so "יש להשתדל שהמספר יהיה באמצע שורה" (try to keep numbers mid-line). This matches the house's leading U+202B.
- **Numbers.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "From 1 to 10, numbers should be written out… Above 10… numerically… When a number begins a sentence, it should always be spelled out… may be broken due to space limitations or reading speed… Measurements should be converted to the metric system unless the original unit of measurement is plot relevant… currency types should be written out", e.g.
    זה עלה 30 דולר.
    ("It cost 30 dollars.")
  - Amazon Hebrew adds: no thousands comma for 1000–9999, a comma from 10,000, no symbols or % sign, the 24-hour clock is allowed, and when the currency isn't named, «80 אלף» ("80 thousand").
  - Qsubs: a one-word number goes in words; big numbers in digits, e.g. «522 אנשים» ("522 people").
- **Names.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "Proper names should be transliterated… Nicknames should be transliterated. Only translate if the nickname conveys a specific meaning relevant to viewers… Names of places and institutions, either real or made up, should be transliterated."
  - Amazon Hebrew: nicknames are translated if they "convey a specific meaning"; "historical/mythical/cartoon character names – Use established… Hebrew-specific translations"; fantasy places are transliterated "unless the name has a specific meaning".
- **Foreign words.** Netflix: translate (hello, merci) or transliterate if there is no translation. Amazon: the same; keep FBI, DNA, GPS in English. Qsubs: integrate known foreign words in transliteration, e.g. «ראיתי שהוצאת את הזבל. מרסי.» ("I saw you took out the trash. Merci.").
- **Honorifics.** No Hebrew guide mentions Japanese honorifics. Netflix's pivot guide requires annotating "honorifics" and "Formality, class" in pivots. Its Creative Excellence page says to "Map the cultural nuances granularly. Eg. … honorifics" ([link](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/11747624732947-Guiding-Principles-of-Creative-Excellence)).
- **Songs.**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "Only subtitle plot-pertinent songs if the rights have been granted… Opening and ending theme songs should only be subtitled if clearly plot pertinent… Treat songs the same way you treat quotations… only question marks and exclamation marks should be used at the end of a line – no commas or periods."
  - Amazon Hebrew: the same, adding that "plot pertinent does not merely mean thematically relevant."
- **On-screen text (forced narratives).**
  - Netflix Hebrew: "Forced narratives (FNs)… should only be included if plot-pertinent… enclosed in hyphens… Never combine a forced narrative with dialogue in the same subtitle… Forced narratives that are redundant… must be deleted." Example:
    - שיקגו, יוני 2013 -
    ("Chicago, June 2013")
  - Amazon Hebrew: the same; "If the camera focuses on a set element with intent, then it is an indication that it is pertinent to the plot."
- **Repetition, fillers, vocatives.**
  - Netflix: translate repeated words once.
  - Amazon: omit fillers ("you know", "well", "okay", "like", sentence-initial "And").
  - ESIST 13: "Obvious repetition of names and common comprehensible phrases need not always be subtitled."

### Inferences
- **[mine]** Our ≤44 characters is 2 more than Netflix and Amazon allow. That is fine for a fan release, and it removes most of the space excuse for literary compaction.
- **[mine]** Honorifics are a house decision (-san → אדון/גברת + name, "Mr./Ms. + name"). No outside guide constrains it.

### Gaps
- No Hebrew guide covers attack-name translation or typeset signs in anime. These are house conventions.

## Actionable rules for our translator

Ranked by expected impact on the "what did I just read?" reaction. Each rule: pattern → why it is unnatural → natural alternative(s) → example. "Support" names the outside source behind the rule; everything else, including all rewrites, is **[mine]**. House conventions (הכל, האלו, זו, יותר מידי, דיי, עלי, nif'al infinitives with yod, -san → אדון/גברת, Ivankov's ווי, translated attack names, 44 characters / 2 lines) are untouched.

**R1. Translate the Japanese function, not the English wording of formulaic lines (relay rule).**
- Pattern: English fan-sub formulas that encode Japanese set phrases:
  - "As expected of X" / "Nothing less than what you'd expect from X" (*sasuga*);
  - "Prepare yourself, world!";
  - "X is convenient for us";
  - "I'm grateful to you".
- Why: the English is already a near-literal pivot of Japanese. Netflix says the target must represent the source language, "not the English pivot file", and that "no literal translations" is non-negotiable. FAR classes the result as source-interference translationese.
- Alternatives: «לא פלא ש…» ("no wonder that…"), «ברור, זה X» ("obviously, it's X"), «מה ציפית, זה X» ("what did you expect, it's X"), «איך לא» ("how could it not"); for "convenient", «משחק לטובתנו» ("plays in our favor"), «בדיוק מה שהיינו צריכים» ("just what we needed"); for "grateful", «אני מודה לך» ("I thank you"), «תודה» ("thanks").
- Examples:
  - «כצפוי מכל כוחות הצי.» ("as expected of all the Navy forces") → «נו, ברור. זה הצי.» ("Well, obviously. It's the Navy.") or «לא סתם קוראים להם הצי.» ("They're not called the Navy for nothing.")
  - «לא פחות ממה שאפשר לצפות ממישהו שהפליג עם מלך הפיראטים!» ("nothing less than one could expect from someone who sailed with the Pirate King") → «לא פלא, הוא הפליג עם מלך הפיראטים!» ("No wonder — he sailed with the Pirate King!")
  - «הבהלה הזאת ממש נוחה לנו.» ("this panic is very convenient for us") → «הבלגן הזה רק משחק לטובתנו.» ("This mess only plays in our favor.")
  - «תתכונן לקראתי, עולם!» ("prepare towards me, world!") → «עולם, תתכונן — אני בא!» ("World, get ready — I'm coming!")
- Support: [Netflix pivot §33](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/219375728-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Templates), [Netflix Creative Excellence](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/11747624732947-Guiding-Principles-of-Creative-Excellence), [Pedersen FAR](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1), Amazon Hebrew §1/§20.

**R2. Replace bookish function words with spoken ones, except in a registered formal voice.**
- Pattern:
  - question words: היכן ("where"), מדוע ("why"), sentence-initial האם (question particle), מנין ("whence");
  - pronoun and time words: אנו ("we"), כעת ("now");
  - negation: אינני / אינך / אינו / אינה / אינם (formal "am/are/is not"), הינו (formal copula);
  - linking words: לקראת ("towards"), אודות ("about"), על מנת ("in order to"), במידה ו ("in the event that"), כה ("so"), אולם / אך ("however" / "but"), ברם ("however"), הנני ("here I am"), שמא ("lest"), טרם ("not yet"), אפוא ("therefore").
- Why: Qsubs names these exact words as never used by ordinary speakers. The Academy calls «אני לא מבין» ("I don't understand", with לא) "completely natural" and «אינני מבין» a written/formal form. Beit Berl calls unneeded raising a common error.
- Alternatives, in the same order:
  - איפה ("where"), למה ("why"), drop האם (the question mark suffices), מאיפה ("from where");
  - אנחנו ("we"), עכשיו ("now");
  - אני לא / אתה לא / הוא לא ("I'm not / you're not / he's not");
  - ל- / מול ("to / facing"), על ("about"), כדי ("in order to"), אם ("if"), כל כך ("so"), אבל ("but"), עוד לא ("not yet"), אז ("so").
- Examples:
  - «מדוע אינך נלחם?» ("Why art thou not fighting?") → «למה אתה לא נלחם?» ("Why aren't you fighting?")
  - «היכן אייס?» ("Where is Ace?", bookish) → «איפה אייס?»
- Exception: characters whose registry entry says "formal/old" may keep some high forms, consistently. Even they should prefer לא in most lines.
- Support: [Qsubs](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907), [Academy/Gadish](https://hebrew-academy.org.il/topic/sheelot_teshuvot/mivharteshuvot/inyan-shel-signon/), [Beit Berl](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/ss-13110774/13110774), [D&R on characters who "stand out"](https://ebrary.net/282604/language_literature/conflicting_priorities_difficult_decisions).

**R3. Swap literary or biblical lexis and abstract nominal style for everyday verbs and established collocations.**
- Pattern: high verbs and nouns, and literary patterns:
  - ציווה ("commanded"), זנח ("abandoned", for giving something up), אסיר תודה ("grateful"), מחזיר ימים ("brings back days");
  - "N רודף N" ("N chases N");
  - collocations that don't exist in Hebrew, such as «תופעות לוואי מכוערות» ("ugly side effects"); the natural collocation is «תופעות לוואי קשות» ("severe side effects").
- Why: this is the "pseudo-spoken / mixed language" of older Hebrew translation (Ben-Shahar). Weissbrod notes that the elevated norm "has almost vanished" in commercial subtitles. The Qsubs principle: use what ordinary people say («כדברי פשוטי העם»), not dictionary words.
- Alternatives: אמר / הורה / ביקש ("said / ordered / asked"), ויתר ("gave up"), מודה ("thank"), איזה זיכרונות ("what memories"), a verbal paraphrase.
- Examples:
  - «אבא ציווה לשמור על האח של אייס בחיים!» ("Father commanded to keep Ace's brother alive!") → «אבא אמר לשמור על אח של אייס בחיים!» ("Pops said to keep Ace's brother alive!")
  - «גילוי רודף גילוי, וכולם מטלטלים.» ("discovery chases discovery, and all are shaking") → «כל גילוי מטלטל יותר מהקודם.» ("Each revelation is more shocking than the last.")
- Support: [Ben-Shahar](https://doi.org/10.7202/037226ar), [Weissbrod](https://doi.org/10.52034/lanstts.v6i.177), [Qsubs](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907), [TED "Modern over traditional"](https://www.ted.com/participate/translate/guidelines).

**R4. Use idioms only in their exact fixed form. Otherwise use plain words, never a half-idiom.**
- Pattern: a near-miss idiom: «נושם בעורף» (literally "breathing on the nape"; the idiom is «נושף בעורף», "blowing on the nape"); «עכבר לכוד הוא דבר מפחיד» ("a trapped mouse is a scary thing"); «יצא לנו מזל» ("we came out with luck").
- Why: a broken idiom is worse than none, because the reader recognizes the template and stumbles on the deviation. That is a garden-path effect, which FAR penalizes as re-reading. Amazon requires "established or standard target-language expressions". TED: if there is no equivalent, choose the "least confusing" plain rendering.
- Alternatives: the dictionary form («נושף בעורף»), a native proverb («גם עכבר בפינה יכול לנשוך», "even a cornered mouse can bite"; «שיחק לנו המזל», "luck played for us"), or a plain statement («כבר ממש קרוב», "already very close").
- Example: «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושם בעורף.» → «שליט הגיהנום כבר נושף לנו בעורף.» ("The ruler of hell is already breathing down our necks.")
- Support: [Amazon Hebrew](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/CooperWebsite/dvp/downloads/AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip), [TED](https://www.ted.com/participate/translate/guidelines), [Pedersen](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1).

**R5. Under space pressure, cut; don't raise the register.**
- Pattern: אנו for אנחנו ("we"), a noun with a possessive suffix (ספינתנו "our-ship", חבריי "my-friends", כוחי "my-power", אבי "my-father"), אינו ("is not"), כעת ("now"), used to fit 44 characters.
- Why: Beit Berl and Transtitles describe this as a known Israeli drift, which Beit Berl warns against "if the register doesn't allow it". The savings are tiny (0–5 characters). The professional toolkits remove content instead.
- Order of cuts:
  1. fillers, vocatives, repetitions;
  2. subject pronouns on past/future verbs («ראיתי», not «אני ראיתי», "I saw");
  3. context-confirming clauses;
  4. reformulate;
  5. split or merge cues.
- Keep colloquial suffix forms: quantifiers (שנינו "both of us", כולנו "all of us"), prepositions (שלי "mine", איתך "with you", בשבילו "for him"), fixed phrases (לדעתי "in my opinion", בעצמי "myself").
- Kinship: «אבא שלי» ("my dad"), not «אבי» ("my father", bookish). For "my brother" use «אח שלי»; «אחי» reads as "bro".
- Example: «ספינתנו לא תשקע!» ("Our-ship shall not sink!") → «הספינה שלנו לא תטבע!» ("Our ship won't sink!")
- Support: [Beit Berl](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/ss-13110774/13110774), [Makor Rishon](https://www.makorrishon.co.il/culture/article/259707), [Netflix templates toolkit](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/219375728-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Templates), [Pedersen on subject deletion](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1), [Qsubs on pronouns](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907).

**R6. Turn English passives and "going to be + participle" into Hebrew active or impersonal-plural forms.**
- Pattern: «X הולך להיות מוצא להורג» ("X is going to be executed"); «הוא נלקח על ידי…» ("he was taken by…").
- Why: Netflix lists "too much passive sentence formation" among the hallmarks of literal translation. Hebrew speech prefers the impersonal plural for unspecified agents.
- Alternatives: «הולכים להוציא את X להורג» ("they're going to execute X"); «לקחו אותו» ("they took him").
- Example: «אייס הולך להיות מוצא להורג» → «הולכים להוציא את אייס להורג» (already in house §1.5; generalize it to all passives).
- Support: [Netflix Creative Excellence](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/11747624732947-Guiding-Principles-of-Creative-Excellence).

**R7. Give each character a register and enforce it (formality table).**
- Pattern: every character speaks the same neutral-high Hebrew.
- Why: Qsubs calls uniform voices "a mistake". Díaz Cintas & Remael: if a character "stand[s] out because of the type of language they speak, this should somehow be reflected". Netflix requires "formality tables" and replicating "tone, register, class, formality".
- Alternative: add a register column to the character registry, e.g.:
  - formal old warrior: high register allowed (e.g., אינני, "I am not");
  - street brawler: colloquial, mild slang;
  - theatrical villain: grand but modern;
  - children: simple.
  Check each line against its speaker's entry.
- Example: the same "Where is he?" becomes, for a formal old warrior,
  «היכן הוא?» (bookish "where" is allowed here)
  and for a brawler,
  «איפה הוא, לעזאזל?» ("Where the hell is he?").
- Support: [Netflix General Requirements §6](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements), [Netflix Hebrew TTSG §20](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide), [D&R](https://ebrary.net/282604/language_literature/conflicting_priorities_difficult_decisions), [Qsubs](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907).

**R8. Calibrate slang and profanity: colloquial, widely understood, and at equivalent severity.**
- Pattern: either sanitized lines («קיימנו יחסי מין» for "we fucked"; «לכי לעזאזל» "go to hell" where the source is mild) or hyper-local or fleeting slang (סבבה "cool", אשכרה "for real", regional terms).
- Why: Netflix and Amazon require "same/equivalent severity and intent… without censoring or toning down", and say "Do not increase the level of perceived obscenity". Amazon: "Do not use regional slangs or idioms that would not be widely understood." Qsubs warns against both sanitizing and "too Israeli" slang.
- Alternative: mainstream colloquial Hebrew. Swear only where the Japanese or English swears, at a matched level.
- Example: "Damn it!" → «לעזאזל!» ("damn it") or «חרא!» ("shit"), depending on severity. Not «אוי ואבוי!» ("oh woe!", archaic exclamation).
- Support: [Netflix TTSG §20](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide), Amazon §20–21, [Netflix Hebrew dubbing PDF](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/9786887342611-Dubbing-Creative-Guidelines-Films-Series-Hebrew), [Qsubs](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907).

**R9. Fix Hebrew-grammar calques of English syntax.**
- Pattern: a preposition not repeated across coordinated nouns; an English-style fronted object («את X ניתן לצפות…», "X one can watch…"); numeral gender or definiteness errors; padding adjectives (שונים "various").
- Why: Netflix §3 do/don't table.
- Alternatives:
  - «ל-X ולחבורתו» ("to X and to his gang");
  - «שלוש בתולות ים» ("three mermaids", correct numeral);
  - drop the padding.
- Example: «הצטרפו לטורבו וחבורתו» → «הצטרפו לטורבו ולחבורתו» ("Join Turbo and his posse").
- Support: [Netflix Hebrew TTSG §3](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide).

**R10. Process gate: a dry read and a read-aloud before shipping.**
- Pattern: lines that pass a side-by-side check against the English.
- Why: Qsubs' «הגהה יבשה» ("dry proofread": read the Hebrew without video or English) exists to catch "Anglicisms". Baños: read dubbing dialogue aloud to check spontaneity. FAR: idiomaticity is judged by "native-like selection".
- Alternative: the blind-reader gate (_NATIVE_REVIEW) reads Hebrew only and asks of each line, "would an Israeli say this, in this character's mouth?"
- Example: «מחזיר ימים» ("returns days") is flagged on a dry read → «איזה זיכרונות» ("what memories").
- Support: [Qsubs](http://www.qsubs.net/index.php?showtopic=44907), [Baños 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750399X.2021.1880262), [Pedersen](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1).

**R11. Detection across the ~340 shipped episodes.**
- Approach:
  - regex lexicon for the R2 word list, with a speaker allow-list from R7;
  - noun-plus-possessive-suffix list for frequent nouns (R5): ספינתנו, חבריי, אבי, אמי, כוחי, ביתי, and similar;
  - English-frame strings (R1, R6): כצפוי מ ("as expected of"), לא פחות ממה ש ("nothing less than"), אסיר תודה ("grateful"), נוחה לנו / נוח לנו ("convenient for us"), «הולך להיות» + participle ("going to be" + participle), לקראתי ("towards me");
  - a broken-idiom list (R4): נושם בעורף and similar;
  - score a stratified sample per arc with FAR-style penalties per subtitle, so the worst arcs are re-reviewed first.
- Why: FAR gives a per-subtitle comparable score. Platform guides prize consistency across a whole project.
- Support: [Pedersen FAR](https://www.jostrans.org/article/download/7801/7458?inline=1), [Netflix TTSG §4 consistency](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide).

**R12. Mechanical consistency (low impact on naturalness, but professional).**
- Numbers 1–10 in words; 11 and up in digits; a number at sentence start in words.
- U+2026 for ellipses; no ellipsis across a continuous split.
- A second-speaker hyphen with no space; one speaker per line.
- No italics.
- Song lyrics only if plot-pertinent, in quotation marks.
- On-screen text in hyphens, never mixed with dialogue in the same cue.
- Support: [Netflix Hebrew TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220636427-Hebrew-Timed-Text-Style-Guide), [Amazon Hebrew guide](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/G/01/CooperWebsite/dvp/downloads/AmazonPrimeVideo_Localization_and_Technical_Style_Guides_September_2026.zip).
